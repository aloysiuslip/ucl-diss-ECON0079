\section*{Abstract}

\begin{center}
    This paper estimates the productivity spillover occuring between colocated firms in the United Kingdom.
    The productivity spillover, a spatial externality, underpins well-established economic phenomena
    around the development of urban clusters in the New Economic Geography literature,
    and is measured and heavily discussed in the evidence on the economic effects of urban density
    measured in the spatial econometrics literature, and underpinning regional growth policy.
    \\[0.9em]
    I develop \itx{prima facie} evidence for the spillover
    by developing a 3-factor production model of Total Factor Productivity (TFP), capital, and labour,
    with TFP in turn decomposed to spillovers of peer TFP, capital, and labour.
    I apply recently-developed econometric techniques for valid identification
    in peer effect group and network settings
    to comprehensive UK firm data from Fame.
    The key to valid identification rests in internal instruments from spatial \itx{exogenous} effects
    in a static model, and from deep lags in a dynamic model:
    this overcomes \cite{Manski1993}'s `Reflection Problem' and the endogeneity of peer effects.
    It requires big data calculations of firm interactions,
    which I use modern data techniques to compute efficiently,
    preserving the advantages of Fame's rich balance sheet microdata
    for both its panel structure and its resulting high-significant estimates.
    \\[0.9em]
    Across my instrumented specifications,
    I evaluate a positive spatial spillover from the labour inputs of a peer firm of \todo{0.4? Calculation is complicated},
    onto any given target firm's productivity.
    I evaluate a milder positive spatial spillover from a larger accumulation of capital inputs from the peer firm.
    The implied spillover from any peer firm's TFP to a target firm is negative,
    \todo{implying that firms compete for local demand(?) and in any contemporaneous period,
    one neighbour's success detracts from their peers}.
    Contrasted with models assuming strict exogeneity across in all peer input decisions,
    which suggest only positive spillovers from peer factors to TFP,
    this suggests the need for caution in devising regional economic policy.
    Spillover effects are moderate and conditional,
    require peer firm sto increase their factor inputs specifically.
    A multiplier effect via a continuous chain of firm-to-firm induced
    positive productivity shocks is less empirically supported.
    \\[0.9em]
    This analysis establishes a baseline framework on which more sophisticated,
    and necessary, extensions to investigate the phenomenon of agglomeration can be built.
    These include the implementation of network and industry fixed effects
    and patterns of measurement to examine the heterogenous strength
    of the productivity spillover, informing how and where agglomerating effects may be strongest.
