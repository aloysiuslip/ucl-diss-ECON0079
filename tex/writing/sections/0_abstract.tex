\section*{Abstract}

\begin{center}
    This paper offers empirical strategies across static and dynamic models to
    estimate the productivity spillover occurring between colocated firms in the United Kingdom.
    The productivity spillover, a spatial externality, underpins well-established economic phenomena
    around the development of urban clusters and regional economic policy.
    \\[0.9em]
    I examine approaches to estimate the spillover using a minimally-restrictive,
    firm-to-firm model. I develop a 3-factor production model of Total Factor Productivity (TFP),
    capital, and labour, with TFP in turn decomposed to spillovers of those same three factors from peers.
    I apply econometric techniques for valid identification
    in peer effect group and network settings to UK mass microdata from Fame.
    The key to valid identification rests in internal instruments from spatial \itx{exogenous} effects
    in a static model, and from deep lags in a dynamic model:
    this overcomes \cite{Manski1993}'s `Reflection Problem' and the endogeneity of peer effects.
    When faced with big data, this requires complex transformations to
    calculate appropriate instruments or fixed effects;
    I show this is possible using modern data techniques.
    \\[0.9em]
    The estimates offered in this paper do not conclude substantive
    estimates of the commonly-reported agglomeration elasticity.
    Instead, I focus on solving several econometric problems for more robust
    estimation on a targeted basis, including the application of
    a peer effects model to a dynamic panel IV setting
    and identifying cases of endogeneity with solutions.
    The evidence provided suggests that static models can be robust,
    and composite instruments have the potential to
    improve efficiency in a dynamic setting.
    \\[0.9em]
    Targeted extensions building on this paper's framework
    include estimation of the heterogenous strength of the spillover effects
    among different sets of actors and the development of network fixed effects.

\end{center}
