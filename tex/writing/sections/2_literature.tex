\section{Literature}
\label{s:literature}

\subsection{Agglomeration and the productivity spillover}
\label{ss:lit-agglom}

Agglomeration is the colocation of firms in spatial regions
for the mutual benefit of a productivity spillover.
It is the imperative force behind the development of cities
(described illustriously in \cite{Goldin2023})
and fundamental to considering alternative dimensions
to the static 2-factor production model underlying most
economics of firm production that mostly intuitively
represents the pattern of growth and clustering
seen in modern economies.
Public attention was drawn to the phenomenon even in the heyday
of the development of classical economics. \cite{Marshall1920}
outlined a series of channels pertinent to increasing urbanisation
around industrial clusters that characterised the late 19th Century across Europe.
Agglomeration was definitively described by \cite{Krugman1991},
an empirical investigation into the clustering of manufacturing firms
using a `core-periphery' model of urban development.
The paper, and the authors' subsequent work of the `New Economic Geography'
field\cite{Krugman1998, Fujita2004, Koo2007}, was nuanced in its characterisation of
`centripetal' forces, grounded in \cite{Marshall1920}'s taxonomy,
accelerating towards a basin of attraction urban area
until they are outweighed by the `centrigual' forces of a developed city:
immobile factors, competition for resources, and congestion.
\par
The movement spurred other considerable advances in spatial economics,
which looked to estimate the raw underlying spatial spillover effect,
supporting `New Economic Geography' contributions which tend to assume
or abstract away from the spillover estimate itself.
Several contributions outlined in \cite{Beeson1991} robustly estimate
the contribution of increased city size to productivity levels;
later work takes this on a more granular level,
developing estimates of the contribution of indices of
spatial and industry density for any given urban area \cite{Glaeser1992,Ellison2010}.
\par
Density-based estimates of the agglomeration `centripetal' force provide
a different interpretation to spillovers from firm-to-firm, both to implications on
economic policy and their own confounding issues.
Contributions at the turn of the 20th century offered some firm-level estimates,
but tended to continue to make significant abstractions,
such as imposing the equilibrium model of firm movement across 2 abstract cities or
the placement of firms all on a 1-dimensional line \cite{Fujita1982, Ciccone1996, Desmet2010}.
Nonetheless, these offer benchmarks to empirical estimates derived and
helpful insights on extending the classical production functions
and deploying distance-decay measures to simulate the spatial characteristic
of knowledge decay or firm-firm interaction barriers, such as in \cite{Lucas2002},
which I emulate in this paper.
\par
Estimates are summarised in \cite{Graham2018}, commissioned for the UK Department for Transport.
They evaluate a mean across 47 international empirical studies around 5\%,
positively signed, though noting the wide variance in estimates.
They additionally lay out a step-by-step framework to estimating firm-to-firm productivity spillovers
through a production function approach. Both group and distance-decay measures are discussed.
Additionally, an overview of the measurable data and econometric options to carrying out an empirical investigation
are comprehensively provided.
This analysis implements their suggestion to employ a dynamic panel IV,
alongside other spatial IV techniques as an instrumentation strategy.
Recent papers utilising these econometric techniques, coupled with exploiting
modern, public, mass data-collection have offered further insightful estimates
on the firm productivity spillover in specific contexts.
\cite{Bloom2013} uses instruments on innovation to isolate `centripetal' and `centrifugal' forces.
\cite{Gruber2023} instruments place-based cost shocks to evaluate the pattern of colocation and productivity of scientists.
There are few estimates that seek to take a UK approach to estimating this spillover factor
on a firm-to-firm level.
\par
\cite{Graham2018} additionally substantively review the econometric pitfalls of estimation
that challenge a spatial econometric investigation at the firm-level.
They highlight the critical issue addressed in this paper
on the simultaneous determination of the dependent variable and the causal productivity spillover,
which causes estimates with a reduced-form TFP residual dependent variable to be
``biased and inconsistent''\footnote{\cite{Graham2018}, p.30}
This paper's advances on this literature are
to move away from aggregated density measures to a firm-to-firm spillover,
address endogeneity via a peer effects model.

\subsection{Peer effects econometrics}
\label{ss:lit-peer-effects-metrics}

The \cite{Manski1993} `Linear-in-Means' model provides a baseline for the econometrics of peer effects.
It provides helpful structure to examining joint behaviour of members of a group or network.
\begin{align*}
    y_i \quad
        &=\quad c \quad
        + \underbrace{\alpha_g}         _{\substack{correlated \\\text{effect}}}
        + \quad\beta x_{i,g}\quad
        + \underbrace{\delta\bar y_{-i,g}}_{\substack{endogenous \\\text{effect}}}
        + \underbrace{\xi\bar x_{-i,g}}   _{\substack{exogenous  \\\text{effect}}}
        + \quad \varepsilon_i
\end{align*}
The model  decomposes spillovers to a direct behavioural, or \itx{endogenous} effect,
$y_{j}\to y_{i}$, where the outcome of interest among peers
affects that of the target individual.
Robust applications of the Manski model demonstrate credible approaches to measuring the \itx{endogenous effect}.
\cite{dustmann-damm2024:neighbourhoods} analyses movers to new neighbourhoods among individuals through
the `Danish Dispersal Policy' of refugee arrivals (1986-1998).
\cite{Chetty2016} measures a similar effect among high-poverty children through the US 1990s `Moving to Opportunity' scheme.
These studies exploit quasi-randomness in the location assignment of individuals and peers
for robust identification, a strength absent from this analysis' time-invariant dataset only partially mitigated by the use of fixed effects
and assumptions of locaiton stickiness.
\par
Additionally, the \cite{Manski1993} model requires contextual or \itx{exogenous} effects,
of which the variation of non-outcome of interest variables among peers
also directly affects that of the target individual, $x_j \to y_i$.
This brings sophistication to the nature of peer spillovers.
A simplified model only allowing endogenous effects through the variable of interest, $y_j \to y_i$
is nested within this structural model, but not identified due to the `Reflection Problem'
and as described in this analysis in section \ref{ss:static-identification}.
In this analysis' firm productivity model, the \itx{exogenous effect}
allows for an additional level of input factor capital and labour
to induce higher target TFP, better management practices or
more efficient combination of factor inputs, perhaps from observing
the mere high level of these factors in their peers,
without the peers themselves necessarily experiencing a productivity increase.
It is additionally a common specification requirement for every individual
to have an own-effect of exogenous characteristics, $x_i \to y_i$,
which corresponds to the standard production function model in this analysis of
$k_{i,t}, l_{i,t}\to y_{i,t}$.
\begin{align*}
    y_{i,g} &= c + \alpha_g + \beta x_{i,g} + \delta\bar y_{-i,g} + \xi\bar x_{-i,g} + \varepsilon_i
    \\
    \bar y_g &= c + \alpha_g + \beta\bar x_g + \delta\bar y_g + \xi\bar x_g + \bar\varepsilon_g
    \\
    (1-\delta)\bar y_g &= c + \alpha_g + (\beta+\xi)\bar x_g + \bar\varepsilon_g
    \\ \tag{large g assumption}
    \bar y_g &= \frac{c + \alpha_g}{1-\delta} +\frac{\beta+\xi}{1-\delta}\bar x_g+\frac{\bar\varepsilon_g}{1-\delta}
        \quad = \bar y_{-i,g}
    \\ \tag{substituting}
    y_{i,g} &= \frac {c + \alpha_g}{1-\delta} +\beta x_i + \frac{\xi+\delta\beta}{1-\delta}\bar x_{-i}
    + \frac\delta{1-\delta}\bar\varepsilon_g + \varepsilon_i 
    \\
    y_{i,g} &= \alpha_g + \pi_0 + \pi_1x_i + \pi_2 \bar x_{-i} + v_g
\end{align*}

The focus of \cite{Manski1993}'s linear-in-means (LMM) contribution is to highlight the `Reflection Problem',
an econometric trap.
The derivation give above shows that the initial structural equation
cannot be estimated directly with OLS due to the endogeneity of $\bar y_{-i,g}: \mathbb E[\bar y_{-i,g}\varepsilon_i]\neq 0$.
Causal variation in $y_{i,g}$ is unidentifiable due to insufficient reduced-form parameters.
\par
A key solution to this is the deployment of instruments, $z_{-i}\propto \bar y_{-i}\ \cap\ z_{-i} \not\propto y_{i}$.
An application employing credibly exogenous instruments for a peer effects measure in an individual crime setting
is given in \cite{dustmann-landerso2024:gangs-babyfathers}.
The equivalent valid instrument in this analysis which is correlated with the peer effect
but has no direct effect on the target outcome of interest
is an additional spatial lag of \itx{exogenous} regressors in the static model.
This means the capital and labour of a firm $k$ who is a `peers of peers': a peer of firm $j$,
but not of firm $i$, the last of which is the target firm of interest.
In this analysis' dynamic model, deep temporal lags $y_{-i,t-2}\propto y_{-i,t}\ \cap\ y_{-i,t-2}\not\propto y_{i, t}$
are also as a valid instrument under an assumption of weak exogeneity of predetermined regressors.
\par
A modern paper, \cite{Bramoulle2009}, explores different identification techniques and their rules for validity
for group and network structures. These structures are more complex than the \cite{Manski1993} model,
which by using a variable of means $\bar y_{-i,g}$, implicitly assumes simple, `leave-one-in' group averages
as the peer \itx{endogenous} regressor.
\par
The paper subsequently explores many complex interaction structures summarised by the weighting matrix $\mathbf W$,
the $n$-dimensional interactions matrix between firms.
I discuss and illustrate structures of $\mathbf W$ in detail in section \ref{ss:data-w-groups}.
The structures of $\mathbf W$ are key to identification.
The contributions from this paper are, in short, that complex $\mathbf W$ matrices which vary group sizes ($s_g$)
and network diameters ($s_d$) are key to discovering valid spatial lag instruments.
Fortunately, these structures resemble realistic interactions common to empirical data,
and are applicable to this analysis' pattern and layout of firm interactions.
This strategy continues to assume exogenous assignment of spatial assignments
and so target-peer interactions.
