\section{Static models}
\label{s:models-static}

I use a stylised 3-factor production model throughout this analysis.
$Y$, $A$, $K$, and $L$ are each firm-year observation's GVA, TFP, labour, and capital in levels.
I denote logged terms with lower-case notation and vectors in bold.
\par
Log TFP, denoted by $z_{i,t}$, is the residual after the own-firm contribution of the two classical inputs.
I decompose it to a constant across all observations, firm and time fixed effects and,
most crucially for this investigation, a vector of the weighted sum of all peer 3-factor production factors
with the dot product against the vector of their respective spillover coefficients $\boldsymbol\theta_0$:
\begin{align}
    \mathbf z_t^{spill} &= \begin{pmatrix}
        \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij}z_{j,t} & \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij}k_{j,t} & \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij}l_{j,t}
    \end{pmatrix}
    \\
    \boldsymbol\theta_0 &= \begin{pmatrix}
        \delta_0 & \xi_0 & \zeta_0
    \end{pmatrix}'
\end{align}
%
Different static weights, $w_{ij}$, are explored throughout this section.
The objective is to estimate the vector of structural coefficients $\boldsymbol\theta_0$
of spillover from peers to any given target firm.
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:static-production-3-factor}
    Y_{i,t} &= A_{i,t} \cdot
                K_{i,t}^{\beta_1} \cdot
                L_{i,t}^{\beta_2}
    \\
    \label{eq:static-tfp-identity}
    z_{i,t} &= \ln(A_{it}) = y_{i,t} - \beta_1 k_{i,t} - \beta_2 l_{i,t}
    \\
    \label{eq:static-main-reduced}
            &= \alpha_i + \gamma_t + \beta_0 + \mathbf z_t^{spill}\boldsymbol\theta_0 + \varepsilon_{i,t}
    \\[0.8em]
    \label{eq:static-main-gva}
    y_{i,t} &= \alpha_i + \gamma_t + \beta_0
                + \beta_1 k_{i,t}
                + \beta_2 l_{i,t}
                \\ &\qquad \notag
                + \delta_0 \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij}z_{j,t}
                + \xi_0 \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij}k_{j,t}
                + \zeta_0 \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij}l_{j,t}
                + \varepsilon_{i,t}
    \\[0.8em] \notag
            &=
                \alpha_i
                + \gamma_t
                + \beta_0
                + \beta_1 k_{i,t}
                + \beta_2 l_{i,t}
                + \varepsilon_{i,t}
                \\ &\qquad \notag
                + \delta_0 \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij} \cdot (y_{j,t}
                - \beta_1 k_{j,t}
                - \beta_2 l_{j,t})
                + \xi_0\sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij}k_{j,t}
                + \zeta_0\cdot \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij}l_{j,t}
    \\[0.8em]
    \label{eq:static-main-peer-effects}
            &=
                \alpha_i
                + \gamma_t
                + \beta_0
                + \beta_1 k_{i,t}
                + \beta_2 l_{i,t}
                + \varepsilon_{i,t}
                \\ &\qquad \notag
                + \delta_0 \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij}y_{j,t}
                + (\xi_0-\delta_0\beta_1) \cdot \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij}k_{j,t}
                + (\zeta_0-\delta_0\beta_2) \cdot \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij}l_{j,t}  
\end{align}
\par
Equation \eqref{eq:static-main-peer-effects} is a linear peer effects specification as given by \cite{Manski1993}.
It is helpful to borrow the terminology from this discussed in section \ref{ss:lit-peer-effects-metrics}
and keep in mind the conceptual distinction between these
and the more general and related concepts of endogeneity, exogeneity, and correlation.
Variables
$\begin{pmatrix}
    k_{i,t} &   l_{i,t}
\end{pmatrix}\in x_{i,t}$
can be thought of as \itx{exogenous} characteristics of firm ${i}$ causally affecting its outcome $y_{i,t}$.
There is an \itx{endogenous} effect directly from peer outcomes $y_{j,t}$ with coefficient $\delta_0$.
Peers also have \itx{exogenous} characteristics $x_{i,t}$,
which have a direct \itx{exogenous effect} on the target's productivity $x_{i,t}$ via $\xi_0$ and $\zeta_0$.
For this section of models, I assume there are no \itx{correlated} effects
and leave further discussion of the implementation of these to \fullref{ss:ext-nfe}.
The use of panel fixed effects has no effect on econometrics
discussed in section \ref{ss:lit-peer-effects-metrics}.
\par
It is important to note that I do not impose restrictions on $\boldsymbol\theta_0$.
I allow each of the 3 factors of production to independently exert a spillover effect in general form.
This allows this analysis' investigation as to which of the production factors matters more for productivity spillovers,
and in what ratio, conceptually linked to the discussion in \ref{ss:lit-marshall}
about identifying the channels through which productivity spillovers might occur.
Through this 3-factor production model, it is hard to link each parameter in $\boldsymbol\theta_0$
to a Marshallian channel, but broadly we might consider $\zeta_0$,
the coefficient on an increase in a peer firm's labour inputs affecting a target's TFP to resemble \cite{Marshall1920}'s
labour pooling, with some interaction to knowledge spillovers,
and $\delta_0$, the coefficient from a peer firm's TFP to target TFP to be the remainder of knowledge spillover effects.
A TFP spillover occurring from peer firm's capital $\xi_0$ would be a novel channel to \cite{Marshall1920}'s discussion
and could reflect the idea of more capital-intensive peers.
Regardless, it is strongly justifiable that we would expect the coefficient on each peer production factor
to spillover to a target firm's production heterogeneously,
and not be proportionate to the contribution of these factors to their own-firm GVA.
I have no \itx{a priori} arguments for the sign of the $\boldsymbol\theta_0$ coefficients
and their relative magnitudes compared to their contributions to own-firm GVA in $\beta_1, \beta_2$.
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:static-alt-gva-yspill}
    y_{i,t}^{(y)} &= \alpha_i + \gamma_t + \beta_0
                + \beta_1 k_{i,t}
                + \beta_2 l_{i,t}
                + \delta_0 \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij}y_{j,t}
                + \varepsilon_{i,t}
    \\
    \label{eq:static-alt-gva-zspill}
    y_{i,t}^{(z)} &= \alpha_i + \gamma_t + \beta_0
                    + \beta_1 k_{i,t}
                    + \beta_2 l_{i,t} 
                    + \delta_0 \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij}z_{j,t}
                    + \varepsilon_{i,t}
    \\ \notag
                &= \alpha_i + \gamma_t + \beta_0
                    + \beta_1 k_{i,t}
                    + \beta_2 l_{i,t} 
                    + \delta_0 \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij}y_{j,t}
                    - \delta_0\beta_1 \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij}k_{j,t}
                    - \delta_0\beta_2 \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij}l_{j,t}
\end{align}
%
Two alternative specifications may be proposed: equation \eqref{eq:static-alt-gva-yspill}
modifies \eqref{eq:static-main-gva} to argue that it is the whole of GVA which matters the peer-target spillover,
not each component heterogeneously. It is trivial to see that this implies the coefficient on the \itx{exogenous effect}
is zero, such that $\xi-\delta_0\beta_1=\zeta-\delta_0\beta_2=0$.
This has the interpretation alluded to above,
that the peer effect of any classical factor, capital and labour,
is exactly in the ratio to its own factor income,
which is a strong restriction to impose.
We might expect this to be merely a nested specification of \eqref{eq:static-main-peer-effects},
for which the hypothesis of the zero coefficients can be tested via Wald-test as a joint hypothesis.
In actual fact, without strict assumptions on the orthogonality of peer errors,
this specification's regressors are fully endogenous, and it falls into
the `Reflection Problem' of identification outlined in section \ref{ss:lit-peer-effects-metrics}.
\par
The alternative in equation \eqref{eq:static-alt-gva-zspill} argues that only the TFP component of production
matters for the spillover to influence a target firm's TFP.
This is also a restriction on the more general \eqref{eq:static-main-gva} such that $\xi_0=\zeta_0=0$.
At first glance, this may appear a feasible restriction,
since it appears this model also follows the structure of the peer effect class of models
and is in fact over-identified,
with both the coefficients of the peer classical factors and $\sum_{j\neq i}w_{ij}y_{j,t}$
identifying $\delta_0$.
However, the next section \ref{ss:static-identification} demonstrates
that the reduced form of this approach yields the need for an \itx{exogeneous effect}
set of restrictions, which coincides with $\xi_0,\zeta_0\neq 0$.
The specification with this TFP-only spillover restriction cannot be identified.
\par
While the classical production function \eqref{eq:static-main-gva}
frequently takes the assumption of constant returns to scale,
$\beta_1 + \beta_2\leq 0$,
no such restrictions for the firm occur with the endogenous decomposition of TFP.
A restriction on logged production \eqref{eq:static-main-gva} collectively on $\beta_1$, $\beta_2$, and $\boldsymbol\theta_0$
is difficult to justify.
As with other external effects, there is no reason to believe that market factor prices
and the firm decision-rule for spatial allocation in view of these spillover effects, $w_{ij}z_{j,t}$, is socially optimal.
A peer firm $j$ with extremely high and growing components of GVA has little interest in siting themselves
in the centre of a cluster of firms that are struggling with TFP growth,
yet the external impacts of that firm would be significant.

\subsection{Static panel identification}
\label{ss:static-identification}

Consistency requires the plausible exogeneity of regressors
used in structural equation \eqref{eq:static-main-peer-effects}
from the error term $\varepsilon_{i,t}$.
Capital and labour inputs in every firm $i,j$ are assumed to be strictly exogenous
to every other firm's TFP error shock, for every period in this static model.
This is crucial for consistency with the direct own-firm $k_{i,t}, l_{i,t}$
inputs, but also the use of $\mathbf w_{ij} k_t, \mathbf W^k l_t$
as an \itx{exogenous} effect and, as we will later see,
the ability to leverage higher-order spatial lags of the \itx{exogenous} effect
as an unconfounded instrument for $w_{ij}y_{j,t}$.
This is a restrictive assumption, but standard in the practice of
constructing static panel models. It is relaxed in Section \ref{s:models-dynamic}.
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:static-identification-factor-exogeneity}
    \mathbb E[
        \varepsilon_{i,t}
        \vert
        \begin{pmatrix}
            k_{i,t} & l_{i,t}
        \end{pmatrix}
    ] &= 0_{(1\times 2)}
    ,\ \forall i, \forall t,\tau
\end{align}

The \itx{endogenous} effect regressor $\sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij} y_{j,t}$ is, unsurprisingly, endogenous,
making valid estimation in this setting more complicated.
To show the endogeneity requires stacking $w_{ij}$ target-peer weights to an $n\times n$ interactions matrix, $\textbf W$.
I invert the \itx{endogenous} component to give a $(\mathbb I-\delta_0\mathbf W)^{-1}$ coefficient on all variables.
\begin{equation}
    \delta_0 < 1 \implies \vert \mathbb I-\delta_0 \mathbf W \vert \neq 0
\end{equation}
The inversion is permissible because I can expect a restriction to apply, ruling out iteratively increasing shocks\footnote{
    $\delta_0\geq1$ would imply an exogenous TFP shock to firm $k$ would
    increase TFP in its peer firm $j$ more than 1x,
    which would increase TFP in that firm's peer $i$ more than 1x,
    and so on until the effect multiplies to infinity,
    or at least on the knife-edge $\delta_0=1$ case,
    any TFP shock occurring in one firm applies equally
    and consistently to every firm in a network.
    Estimating $\delta_0$ is a helpful indicator of a multicollinear model
    with unstable coefficient estimates.
}. We can interpret this as a `spatial lag polynomial', directly comparable
to the lag polynomial and MA inversion procedure in autoregressive time series analysis\footnote{
    Note that vectors $\mathbf k_t, \mathbf l_t$ appear twice in \eqref{eq:static-reflection-problem},
    as components of $\mathbf y_t$ so coefficients can be grouped
    but it is clearer to separate this into an 'own-firm'
    effect of $\mathbf k_t, \mathbf l_t$ and a spillover effect of
    $\mathbf W^{k+1}k_t, \mathbf W^{k+1}l_t\ \forall k\geq 0$
}.
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:static-vector-reduced}
    \mathbf y_t &= \boldsymbol \alpha
                    + \gamma_t \boldsymbol \iota
                    + \beta_0 \boldsymbol \iota
                    + \beta_1 \mathbf k_{t}
                    + \beta_2 \mathbf l_{t}
                    \\ & \qquad\quad \notag
                    + \delta_0 \mathbf W \mathbf y_t
                    + (\xi_0-\delta_0\beta_1) \mathbf W \mathbf k_t
                    + (\zeta_0-\delta_0\beta_2) \mathbf W \mathbf l_t 
                    + \boldsymbol \varepsilon_{t}
    \\[1em] \notag
            &= (\mathbb I - \delta_0 \mathbf W)^{-1} \big(
                    \boldsymbol \alpha
                    + \gamma_t \boldsymbol \iota
                    + \beta_0 \boldsymbol \iota
                    + \beta_1 \mathbf k_{t}
                    + \beta_2 \mathbf l_{t}
                    \\ & \qquad\quad \notag
                    + (\xi_0-\delta_0\beta_1) \mathbf W \mathbf k_t
                    + (\zeta_0-\delta_0\beta_2) \mathbf W \mathbf l_t 
                    + \boldsymbol \varepsilon_{t}
                \big)
    \\[1em]
            &= \sum_{k=0}^\infty \delta_0^k \mathbf W^k \big(
                    \boldsymbol \alpha
                    + \gamma_t \boldsymbol \iota
                    + \beta_0 \boldsymbol \iota
                    + \beta_1 \mathbf k_{t}
                    + \beta_2 \mathbf l_{t}
                    \\ & \qquad\quad \notag
                    + (\xi_0-\delta_0\beta_1) \mathbf W \mathbf k_t
                    + (\zeta_0-\delta_0\beta_2) \mathbf W \mathbf l_t 
                    + \boldsymbol \varepsilon_{t}
                \big)
    \\[1em]
    \label{eq:static-reflection-problem}
            &=   \sum_{k=0}^\infty \delta_0^k \mathbf W^k
                        \boldsymbol \alpha
                    +\frac{\gamma_t + \beta_0}{1-\delta_0}\boldsymbol \iota
                    + \beta_1 \mathbf k_{t}
                    + \beta_2 \mathbf l_{t}
                    \\ & \qquad\quad \notag
                    + \xi_0\sum_{k=0}^\infty \delta_0^k \mathbf W^{k+1} \mathbf k_t
                    + \zeta_0\sum_{k=0}^\infty \delta_0^k \mathbf W^{k+1} \mathbf l_t                    
                    + \sum_{k=0}^\infty \delta_0^k \mathbf W^k
                        \boldsymbol \varepsilon_{t}
    \\[1em]
    \label{eq:static-red-endogenous-effect}
    y_{j,t}     &= \dots
                    + \underbrace{
                        \sum_{k=0}^\infty
                        \left[
                            \delta_0^k \mathbf W^{k+1}
                        \right]_{(j,)}
                        \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf k_t & \mathbf l_t\end{pmatrix}
                        \begin{pmatrix}
                            \xi_0       \\
                            \zeta_0
                        \end{pmatrix}
                    }_{\text{exogenous}_j}
                    + \underbrace{
                        \sum_{k=0}^\infty
                        [\delta_0^k \mathbf W^k]_{(j,)} \cdot
                    \boldsymbol \varepsilon_t
                    }_{\text{error transformation}_j}
\end{align}

It is the primary econometric challenge of this section to overcome the endogeneity of $y_{j,t}$,
which is clearly demonstrated in reduced-form equation \eqref{eq:static-red-endogenous-effect}.
It contains a linear transformation of all firms' error shocks $\boldsymbol \varepsilon_{t}$,
including $\varepsilon_{i,t}$. This implies:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb E[y_{j,t}\varepsilon_{i,t}]\neq 0
\end{equation}
\par
The `Reflection Problem' poses a second issue as outlined in \cite{Manski1993}.
Equation \eqref{eq:static-reflection-problem} shows that $\mathbf W \mathbf y_t$,
is a linear combination of all other regressors. In the classical setup,
$\mathbf W$ is a block diagonal matrix of equal-size groups,
and \cite{Manski1993}, later reaffirmed in \cite{Bramoulle2009}
show that the reduced-form estimation of this model contains fewer estimated parameters
than structural parameters, making variation in $y_{j,t}$ indistinguishable
to that of all other regressors.
\par
There are two solutions. I could either proceed with an extremely strong exogeneity assumption,
\eqref{eq:static-ass-ma-exogeneity}, which is most likely satisfied by total orthogonality
between all contemporaneous idiosyncratic errors hitting peer firms \eqref{eq:static-ass-error-orthogonality}.
This in turn makes the regressor $y_{j,t}$ exogenous,
allowing direct estimation of \eqref{eq:static-main-gva}, sidestepping the `Reflection Problem'
by assuming away \itx{endogenous} regressors, and having each regressor exogenously identified in itself.
For identification, this implies that any productivity shock $\varepsilon_{j,t}$ occurring in peer firm $j$,
if it is contemporaneously correlated with TFP ${z_i,t}$ in target firm $i$
must be attributable to the peer effect coefficient via  $\delta_0$.
This precludes any contemporaneous, correlated independent TFP shocks,
which might occur on both the global and local level.
Model (1.) takes this assumption in \ref{ss:model-lmm}.
We should assess these results with the heavy caveat that this strict error orthogonality
assumption is unlikely to be credible.
\begin{align}
    \notag
    \mathbb E[
        \varepsilon_i
        \vert
        \boldsymbol W^k
        \begin{pmatrix}
            \boldsymbol\alpha           &
            \gamma_t \boldsymbol\iota   &
            \beta_0\boldsymbol\iota     &
            \boldsymbol k_t             &
            \boldsymbol l_t             
        \end{pmatrix}
    ] &=0,\  \forall k
    \\
    \label{eq:static-ass-ma-exogeneity}
    \mathbb E[
        \varepsilon_{i,t}
        \vert
        \boldsymbol W^k
        \boldsymbol \varepsilon_t
    ] &=0,\  
    \forall i,j
    \\
    \label{eq:static-ass-error-orthogonality}
    \impliedby \mathbb E[\varepsilon_{i,t}\varepsilon_{j,t}] &= 0,\ \forall k
\end{align}

Instead, as recommended in \cite{Bramoulle2009},
the most defensible approach is to internal instrument the \itx{endogenous} variable, $y_{j,t}$
through deeper spatial lags of the \itx{exogenous} parameters
$\mathbf W^2\mathbf x_t, \mathbf W^3\mathbf x_t \dots \mathbf W^k \mathbf x_t\ \forall k>2$.
These are valid instruments under certain testable properties of $\mathbf W$ given in \tablerefp{tab:bdf_identification}.
Essentially, these reduce to checking that the matrices $\mathbb I, \mathbf W, \mathbf W^2$
are linearly independent, which are sufficiently achieved for group interactions by
having 2 or more differently-sized groups, or a network diameter of at least 2.
\par
This identification strategy is not a `free lunch'.
It requires the underlying assumption that the group size, or network diameter,
is in itself exogenously determined. This would be intuitively violated,
if firms colocate based on the expected productivity benefits of moving to an area,
namely to benefit from the supply-side spillovers we aim to investigate.
Unfortunately, it is highly likely that firm location decisions are endogenous,
this is the whole principle of the \nameref{ss:lit-neg} literature whose
descriptive patterns of firm movements underlying productivity spillover I here aim to investigate.
I am helped here, however, by the use of time-invariant weights.
Plausibly, over the observations of a panel dataset up to 20 years,
if firm weights apply to each firm on a fixed basis,
the temporal variation despite these constant location effects provide credible exogenous variation.
On this however, the strategy runs into the limitations of Fame location data discussed in \ref{ss:data-limitations}.
The analysis is also helped by the relatively higher number of panel observations
to relatively low ratio of confounding movers in the dataset,
as well as the numerous, plausibly exogenous reasons why a firm might choose 
to set up in a location with path dependency, rather than to fully maximise
its peer spillover benefits.
On the balance of arguments, I take the assumption of network exogeneity
and identification approach for the group models in Table \ref{tab:lmm_basic_str}.
\par
I use the instruments $\mathbf W^2\mathbf x_t$ and other second-order variants
for this section's group models \ref{tab:lmm_basic_str}
and $\mathbf W^2\mathbf x_t, \mathbf W^3\mathbf x_t, \mathbf W^4$
as feasible instruments for this section's network models in \tablerefp{tab:dd-basic-str}.
Equation \eqref{eq:static-moment-conditions} illustrates the valid $2l$ moment conditions,
noting that I have chosen to display this in a $l \times 2$ matrix for space and conceptual symmetry,
as the instruments can be grouped by $k_t$ and $l_t$ regressors.
The first 2 rows correspond to the exogenous regressors within the reduced-form equation itself,
the own-firm classical factors effect from $k_{i,t},l_{i,t}$
and the \itx{exogenous} peer effect from $k_{j,t},l_{j,t}$.
The remaining rows are instrument linear combinations of all
firm classical factor inputs $\mathbf k_t, \mathbf l_t$,
using the higher-order spatial lags of $\mathbf W$ discussed.
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:static-moment-conditions}
    \mathbb E[
        \epsilon_{i,t}\ 
        \vert
        \begin{pmatrix}
            k_{i,t}                                     & l_{i,t} \\
            w_{ij}k_{j,t}                               & w_{ij}l_{j,t}   \\
            \left[\mathbf W^2\right]_{(j,)} \mathbf k_t & \left[\mathbf W^2\right]_{(j,)} \mathbf l_t\\
            \left[\mathbf W^3\right]_{(j,)} \mathbf k_t & \left[\mathbf W^3\right]_{(j,)} \mathbf l_t\\
            \vdots & \vdots \\
            \left[\mathbf W^k\right]_{(j,)} \mathbf k_t & \left[\mathbf W^k\right]_{(j,)} \mathbf l_t
        \end{pmatrix}
    ]
    &= 0_{l\times 2}
\end{align}

These instruments and conditions are intuitive:
we can see through equation \eqref{eq:static-red-endogenous-effect} that
a linear combination of all firms' contemporaneous capital and labour inputs,
$\mathbf W^{k+1}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf k_t & \mathbf l_t\end{pmatrix}\ \forall k\geq0$ 
are an exogenous component of $y_{j,t}$ with the coefficients $\xi_0$ and $\zeta_0$ respectively.
It has relevance, meaning $\mathbb E[y_{j,t}\cdot [\mathbf W^k]_{(j,)}\mathbf x_t]\neq 0,\ \forall k>2$
as long as $\xi_0\neq 0$ and $\zeta_0 \neq 0$,
which occurs in all unrestricted cases distinct from \eqref{eq:static-alt-gva-zspill}.
The relevance conditions themselves are also unsurprising and highly intuitive
given the model's setup with GVA equation \eqref{eq:static-main-gva}.
Note that the relevance conditions are more complex setup in \cite{Bramoulle2009},
this is because the factor $\delta_0 \begin{pmatrix}\beta_1 & \beta_2\end{pmatrix}$
cancels out due the accounting composition of TFP in the \itx{exogenous effect}, which is absent from the general model.
This also allows clear understanding of why the TFP-only spillover model \eqref{eq:static-alt-gva-zspill} fails,
as setting both $\xi_0=\zeta_0=0$ means the higher order spatial lags of $\mathbf k_t$ and $\mathbf l_t$
disappears from the composition of the $y_{j,t}$ term.
An Anderson-Rubin test for weak instruments in my nested 3-factor spillover setup
can test for the strength of instrument of $\mathbf k_t$ and $\mathbf l_t$.
If I find that the higher-order spatial lags are jointly weak instruments,
that would validate the TFP-only spillover model and suggest $\xi_0=\zeta_0=0$.
\par
Finally, one might observe that $[\mathbf W]_{(j,)}\mathbf x_t$
is a component of $y_{j,t}$ and so a valid instrument.
However, it is does not increase the number of instruments I would use in my instrumental specification
as it is already included as an exogenous regressor in \ref{eq:static-vector-reduced}
so does not help increase our identification of $y_{j,t}$.

\subsection{Linear-in-means grouped model}
\label{ss:model-lmm}

The initial model proceeds with the strict error orthogonality
\eqref{eq:static-ass-ma-exogeneity} and \eqref{eq:static-ass-error-orthogonality}
assumptions. This is helpful as a benchmark to compare the IV strategy,
with more credible assumptions, that I then adopt.
Model (1.) estimates \eqref{eq:static-lmm-reduced-estimated},
noting that the single group interactions matrix $\mathbf W_{g}$ corresponds to the \itx{pc8} group.
\begin{align}
    \eqreref{eq:static-main-peer-effects}
    y_{i,t} &= \alpha_i + \gamma_t + \beta_0 + \beta_1 k_{i,t} + \beta_2 l_{i,t} + \varepsilon_{i,t}
        \\ \notag
            &\qquad
                    + \delta_0 \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij}y_{j,t}
                    + (\xi_0-\delta_0\beta_1) \cdot \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij,g}k_{j,t}
                    + (\zeta_0-\delta_0\beta_2) \cdot \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij,g}l_{j,t}
    \\ \label{eq:static-lmm-reduced-estimated}
    y_{i,t} &= \alpha_i
                    + \gamma_t
                    + \pi_0
                    + \pi_1 k_{i,t}
                    + \pi_2 l_{i,t}
                    \\ &\qquad \notag
                    + \pi_3 \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij,g}y_{i,t}
                    + \pi_4 \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij,g}k_{j,t}
                    + \pi_5 \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij,g}l_{j,t}
                    + \varepsilon_{i,t}
    \\
    \mathbb E[ &\varepsilon_{i,t} \cdot
        \begin{pmatrix}
            k_{i,t} &
            l_{i,t} &
            y_{j,t} &
            k_{j,t} &
            l_{j,t}
        \end{pmatrix}'
    ] = 0_{(5\times 1)}
    \\ &\qquad
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \hat\pi_0     \\
        \hat\pi_1     \\
        \hat\pi_2     \\
        \hat\pi_3     \\
        \hat\pi_4     \\
        \hat\pi_5
    \end{pmatrix}
    =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \beta_0     \\
        \beta_1     \\
        \beta_2     \\
        \delta_0     \\
        \xi_0-\delta_0\beta_1     \\
        \zeta_0-\delta_0\beta_2
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{align}

With 5 structural and 5 reduced-form parameters, this model is identified.
The initial static model applies simple time-invariant weights $w_{ij}$
in a group structure, $\mathbf W_{g1}$
I discussed group assignment in section \ref{ss:data-distance}.
\par
Model (2) instruments these groups via $\mathbf W^2\mathbf x$,
The reduced-form equation is given in \eqref{eq:static-lmm-iv-fd-reduced-estimated}.
The IV strategy has the moment conditions in \eqref{eq:static-moment-conditions},
and estimates parameters using continuously-updating Generalised Method of Moments (GMM-CUE),
of calculating an efficient transformation matrix to weigh moment conditions \eqref{eq:static-moment-conditions}
to improve the estimator's efficiency.
All the IV models in this analysis are overidentified, as helpfully the structural model
has 2 \itx{exogenous} parameters in capital and labour for 1 \itx{endogenous} GVA term.
In the group model, I present additional instruments up to the 3rd-order and 4th-order lags,
as the first-stage 2SLS results presented in Appendix \ref{app:static-groups} Table \ref{tab:lmm_ols}
suggests a considerable jump in adjusted-R$^2$ when moving from 3rd-order to 4th-order lag instruments.
\begin{align}
    \eqreref{eq:static-main-peer-effects}
    y_{i,t} &= \alpha_i + \gamma_t + \beta_0 + \beta_1 k_{i,t} + \beta_2 l_{i,t} + \varepsilon_{i,t}
        \\ \notag
            &\qquad
                    + \delta_0 \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij,g}y_{j,t}
                    + (\xi_0-\delta_0\beta_1) \cdot \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij,g}k_{j,t}
                    + (\zeta_0-\delta_0\beta_2) \cdot \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij,g}l_{j,t}
    \\
    \label{eq:static-lmm-iv-fd-reduced-estimated}
    \Delta y_{i,t} &= \Delta\gamma_t
                    + \beta_1 \Delta k_{i,t}
                    + \beta_2 \Delta l_{i,t}
                    \\ &\qquad \notag
                    + \beta_3 \Delta \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij,g}y_{i,t}
                    + \beta_4 \Delta \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij,g}k_{j,t}
                    + \beta_5 \Delta \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij,g}l_{j,t}
                    + \Delta \varepsilon_{i,t}
    \\
    \eqreref{eq:static-moment-conditions}
    \mathbb E &[
        \epsilon_{i,t}\ 
        \vert
        \begin{pmatrix}
            k_{i,t}                                     & l_{i,t} \\
            w_{ij}k_{j,t}                               & w_{ij}l_{j,t}   \\
            \left[\mathbf W^2_g\right]_{(j,)} \mathbf k_t & \left[\mathbf W^2_g\right]_{(j,)} \mathbf l_t\\
            \left[\mathbf W^3_g\right]_{(j,)} \mathbf k_t & \left[\mathbf W^3_g\right]_{(j,)} \mathbf l_t\\
            \vdots & \vdots \\
            \left[\mathbf W^k_g\right]_{(j,)} \mathbf k_t & \left[\mathbf W^k_g\right]_{(j,)} \mathbf l_t
        \end{pmatrix}
    ]
    = 0_{l\times 2}
\end{align}

As a brief extension, I explore regressing multiple, non-overlapping spatial groups at once.
This is a natural examination of the specification as
I already have the $\mathbf W_{g1}, \mathbf W_{g2}, \mathbf W_{g3}$ concentric doughnut group transformations
for \itx{pc8}, \itx{pc4} and \itx{ttwa} to run all in the panel IV model at once.
The algebraic derivation of endogeneity and valid instruments
is very similar to those in the single-regressor problem,
where the inversion term is the sum matrices weighted by their coefficient $\delta_s$.

\begin{align}
    \mathbf y_t &= \boldsymbol \alpha
                    + \gamma_t \boldsymbol \iota
                    + \beta_0 \boldsymbol \iota
                    + \beta_1 \mathbf k_{t}
                    + \beta_2 \mathbf l_{t}
                    \\ & \quad \notag
                    + \sum_{s=1}^3 \delta_s \mathbf W_{gs} \mathbf y_t
                    + \sum_{s=1}^3 (\xi_s-\delta_s\beta_1) \mathbf W_{gs} \mathbf k_t
                    + \sum_{s=1}^3 (\zeta_s-\delta_s\beta_2) \mathbf W_{gs} \mathbf l_t 
                    + \boldsymbol \varepsilon_{t}
    \\[1em] \notag
            &= (\mathbb I - \sum_{s=1}^3\delta_s \mathbf W_{gs})^{-1} \big(
                    \boldsymbol \alpha
                    + \gamma_t \boldsymbol \iota
                    + \beta_0 \boldsymbol \iota
                    + \beta_1 \mathbf k_{t}
                    + \beta_2 \mathbf l_{t}
                    + \boldsymbol \varepsilon_{t}
                    \\ & \quad \notag
                    + (\xi_0-\delta_0\beta_1) \mathbf W \mathbf k_t
                    + (\zeta_0-\delta_0\beta_2) \mathbf W \mathbf l_t 
                \big)
    \\[1em]
            &=   \sum_{k=0}^\infty(\sum_{s=1}^3\delta_s \mathbf W_{gs})^k
                        \boldsymbol \alpha
                    +\frac{\gamma_t + \beta_0}{1-\sum_{s=1}^3\delta_s}\boldsymbol \iota
                    + \sum_{k=0}^\infty (\sum_{s=1}^3\delta_s \mathbf W_{gs})^k
                        (\beta_1 \mathbf k_{t} + \beta_2 \mathbf l_{t})
                    \\ & \quad \notag
                    + \sum_{k=0}^\infty
                        (\sum_{s=1}^3 \delta_s \mathbf W_{gs})^k
                        \sum_{s=1}^3 \mathbf W_{gs}
                        \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf k_t & \mathbf l_t\end{pmatrix}
                        \begin{pmatrix}
                            \xi_s-\delta_s\beta_1       \\
                            \zeta_s-\delta_s\beta_2
                        \end{pmatrix}
                    + \sum_{k=0}^\infty (\sum_{s=1}^3\delta_s \mathbf W_{gs})^k
                        \boldsymbol \varepsilon_{t}
    \\[1em]
    \label{eq:static-3r-endogenous-effect}
    y_{j,t}     &= \dots
                    + \underbrace{
                        \sum_{k=0}^\infty
                            \left[
                            (\sum_{s=1}^3 \delta_s \mathbf W_{gs})^k
                            \sum_{s=1}^3 \mathbf W_{gs}
                            \right]_{(j,)}
                            \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf k_t & \mathbf l_t\end{pmatrix}
                            \begin{pmatrix}
                                \xi_s-\delta_s\beta_1       \\
                                \zeta_s-\delta_s\beta_2
                            \end{pmatrix}
                    }_{\text{exogenous}_j}
                    \\ & \quad\ \ \quad \notag
                    + \sum_{k=0}^\infty
                        [
                            (\sum_{s=1}^3\delta_s \mathbf W_{gs})^k
                        ]_{(j,)} \cdot
                    \boldsymbol \varepsilon_t
\end{align}

This strategy of multiple weighting groups at once
opens up interesting additional cross-transformation instruments.
These cross-transformations are possible
and create new, linearly independent regressors
as long as there is sufficient heterogeneity between the weighting matrices,
which here is theoretically guaranteed by the mutual orthogonality of $\mathbf W_g$.
Any combination of higher-order $\mathbf W_{gs}$ group transformations
can be employed as instruments \eqref{eq:static-3r-x-instr},
or the linear composite of the 3 group transformation matrices,
which intuitively represents all firms in a \itx{ttwa}
can be used in higher orders \eqref{eq:static-3r-sum-instr}.
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:static-3r-x-instr}
    \mathbb E &[
        \epsilon_{i,t}\ 
        \vert
        \begin{pmatrix}
            \left[\mathbf W_{g1}\mathbf W_{g2}\right]_{(j,)} \mathbf k_t & \left[\mathbf W_{g1}\mathbf W_{g2}\right]_{(j,)} \mathbf l_t\\
            \left[\mathbf W_{g2}\mathbf W_{g3}\right]_{(j,)} \mathbf k_t & \left[\mathbf W_{g2}\mathbf W_{g3}\right]_{(j,)} \mathbf l_t\\
            \vdots & \vdots \\
        \end{pmatrix}
    ]
    = 0
    \\
    \label{eq:static-3r-sum-instr}
    \mathbb E &[
        \epsilon_{i,t}\ 
        \vert
        \begin{pmatrix}
            \left[\sum_{s=1}^3\mathbf W_{gs}\mathbf W_{g1}\right]_{(j,)} \mathbf k_t
            & \left[\sum_{s=1}^3\mathbf W_{gs}\mathbf W_{g1}\right]_{(j,)} \mathbf l_t
            \\
            \left[\sum_{s=1}^3\mathbf W_{gs}\mathbf W_{g2}\right]_{(j,)} \mathbf k_t
            & \left[\sum_{s=1}^3\mathbf W_{gs}\mathbf W_{g2}\right]_{(j,)} \mathbf l_t
            \\
            \left[\sum_{s=1}^3\mathbf W_{gs}\mathbf W_{g3}\right]_{(j,)} \mathbf k_t
            & \left[\sum_{s=1}^3\mathbf W_{gs}\mathbf W_{g3}\right]_{(j,)} \mathbf l_t
        \end{pmatrix}
    ]
    = 0_{3\times 2}
\end{align}

Importantly, the reduced form in \eqref{eq:static-3r-endogenous-effect}
shows that the coefficients on the \itx{exogenous effect} regressors
are no longer merely $\xi_0, \zeta_0$, but the linear combination given.
This model therefore provides a theoretical route to identifying
and testing \eqref{eq:static-alt-gva-zspill}, the z-spillover model,
should that be the researcher's preferred specification.
\par
However, in practice, regressing more groups
proves unlikely to offer more insights with this dataset and holistic approach
without additional diagnosis.
Coefficients reported in Appendix \ref{app:static-groups} Table \ref{tab:lmm_3r_str}
appear unstable, though this is slightly alleviated by omitting the outer \itx{ttwa}
group and regressing only using \itx{pc8} and \itx{pc4} $\mathbf W_{g}$ transformations.
Nevertheless, in the results given here,
these fail the standard Sargan-Hansen test for overidentifying restrictions.
In addition, employing more instruments, the higher-order cross-transformations of $\mathbf W_{gs}$
discussed is theoretically sound, but led to multicollinearity in this dataset
through any approach. This could be due to the fact that each $\mathbf W_{gs}$
is not truly independent and orthogonal, the identities of \itx{pc8} and \itx{pc4} are linked.
\par
This extension is interesting and useful because the assignment of multiple groups,
especially through more sophisticated dimensions as discussed in section \ref{ss:param-select-xvalidate}
through industry and regional cuts,
could lead to better understanding of firm's heterogenous peer groups.
Perhaps these alternative group choices will have more success
by producing independent, orthogonal matrices
though they run beyond the scope of the simple spatial group identities
selected here.

\subsection{Static distance decay}
\label{ss:model-dd}

I employ a similar static specification strategy,
but employing $\mathbf W_d$ distance-decayed weights in a network structure,
rather than group weights.
The reciprocal distance weights are stylistic of a declining peer influence further away,
representing a lower frequency of social collisions that might inspire knowledge spillovers,
barriers created via transport and time costs,
and decreased ease of peer observation.
Similarly, if this analysis' hypothesis is that spatial proximity
is key to determination of a peer group,
it is intuitive that increasing distance, as a continuous measure,
would imply a decline in influence, rather than equal influence of all peers in an arbitrary cut-off.
\par
I estimate this model employing the spatial lag instruments only,
dropping the error orthogonality variant.
The reduced-form equation and moment conditions of these models
are identical to those of the group setting, except for the use of
different weights which show up in $w_{ij,d}$ and $\mathbf W_d$.
\begin{align}
    \eqreref{eq:static-main-peer-effects}
    y_{i,t} &= \alpha_i + \gamma_t + \beta_0 + \beta_1 k_{i,t} + \beta_2 l_{i,t} + \varepsilon_{i,t}
        \\ \notag
            &\qquad
                    + \delta_0 \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij}y_{j,t}
                    + (\xi_0-\delta_0\beta_1) \cdot \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij,g}k_{j,t}
                    + (\zeta_0-\delta_0\beta_2) \cdot \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij,g}l_{j,t}
    \\
    \Delta y_{i,t} &= \Delta\gamma_t
                    + \beta_1 \Delta k_{i,t}
                    + \beta_2 \Delta l_{i,t}
                    \\ &\qquad \notag
                    + \beta_3 \Delta \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij,d}y_{i,t}
                    + \beta_4 \Delta \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij,d}k_{j,t}
                    + \beta_5 \Delta \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij},dl_{j,t}
                    + \Delta \varepsilon_{i,t}
    \\
    \eqreref{eq:static-moment-conditions}
    \mathbb E &[
        \epsilon_{i,t}\ 
        \vert
        \begin{pmatrix}
            k_{i,t}                                     & l_{i,t} \\
            w_{ij}k_{j,t}                               & w_{ij,d}l_{j,t}   \\
            \left[\mathbf W_d^2\right]_{(j,)} \mathbf k_t & \left[\mathbf W_d^2\right]_{(j,)} \mathbf l_t\\
            \left[\mathbf W_d^3\right]_{(j,)} \mathbf k_t & \left[\mathbf W_d^3\right]_{(j,)} \mathbf l_t\\
            \vdots & \vdots \\
            \left[\mathbf W_d^k\right]_{(j,)} \mathbf k_t & \left[\mathbf W_d^k\right]_{(j,)} \mathbf l_t
        \end{pmatrix}
    ]
    = 0_{l\times 2}
\end{align}
