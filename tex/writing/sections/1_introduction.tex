\section{Introduction}
\label{s:intro}

The strength of the spatial productivity spillover
is crucial to policy and economic discussions on growth, production,
and the formation of urban clusters, but it is notoriously tricky to
estimate\footnote{\cite{Krugman1998}, \cite{Fujita2004}}.
The purpose of this paper is to demonstrate that modern advances in
rich microdata compilation, computing, and econometric theory and techniques
mean that progress is possible in this decades-long empirical problem.
Indeed, evidence is derived by stripping away restrictions
and stylised patterns of firm and household behaviour that simplify
the problem in empirical literature,
in favour of estimation with a stylised, classical model
only using features available from firm data.
This provides a baseline technique for future, more sophisticated models.
The analysis offers four key contributions that are substantively novel:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The development of data science techniques to calculate spatial transformations
            on rich microdata to fulfil exogenous regression specifications
    \item The application of higher-order spatial lags as an internal instrument
            deploying a peer effects model in a firm productivity setting
    \item The application of higher-order temporal lags as an instrument
            in a dynamic panel peer effects model,
            contrasting instrument performance
    \item Empirical estimates of the firm-to-firm spatial productivity spillover.
    This is evidence that can underlie patterns of firm colocation discussed
    in the more sophisticated urban development literature,
    and provides a framework for future extensions to investigate heterogenous effects.
\end{enumerate}
\par % Section 2: literature
This analysis acknowledges a large body of work on agglomeration
which elucidates the importance of
urban density and development on firm productivity.
In section 2, I give an overview of the substantive contributions from
urban economics and New Economic Geography (NEG),
which explain agglomeration driver channels
and offer sophisticated approaches to modelling to household and firm dynamic location choices.
No attempt is made to recreate this whole body of work.
Instead, this analysis abstracts away from the urban modelling choices
common to this literature. Despite being a spatial analysis,
location is employed only as a feature to identify a group of spatial peers for every firm.
From this mere spatial proximity, this analysis reviews approaches to generating empirical evidence
on the pure productivity spillover from peers to a target firm,
highlighting approaches to overcome empirical challenges on the way.
If successful, evidence derived from this framework may provide \itx{prima facie}
support for the centrifugal force driving urbanisation and cluster formation.
This is crucial to both the descriptive analysis of economic geography
and the prescription of regional policy.
\par
Section 2 also gives an overview of the econometric literature
on peer effects, from \cite{Manski1993}'s `Reflection Problem'
to evaluating instrumental validity in a more complex graph and network setting in \cite{Bramoulle2009}.
This analysis applies that econometric theory to the productivity spillover setting.
\par
In sections 3 and 4, I discuss the handling of data from Fame,
a compilation of publicly-available, balance-sheet data from UK companies
designed for comparative research. The cleaned Fame dataset
provides more than 150,000 firms' data and 1.1 million panel observations.
Section 3 also highlights data science techniques developed for this paper
to undertake complex matrix transformations of higher-order spatial lags of
an $(n\times n)$ interactions matrix necessary to compute valid instruments
and eliminate endogeneity.
I provide a detailed guide to reproducing methods in Appendix \ref{app:data-cleaning},
with all code available publicly\footnote{https://github.com/aloysiuslip/ucl-diss-ECON0079}.
\par
In section 5, I give the econometric analysis of a static model with spatial peer effects,
conceptualisable as `spatial lags' of the classical regressors in a 3-factor production function.
This contrasts the merits of different weighting approaches to analysing peer groups
and the use of spatial lags as valid instruments to the peer effects \itx{endogenous} regressor.
I estimate both `groups' models, where the strength of a productivity spillover
is the average of nearby neighbours, and `networks' models,
which applies a distance-decayed individualised weight for every target-peer firm pair.
\par
In section 6, I expand the use of internal instruments to temporal lags.
This builds on modern `System GMM' methods from \cite{Blundell1998}.
I discuss advantages of relaxing strict assumptions to `weak exogeneity'
and evaluate the predetermined nature of classical production factors in a dynamic setting.
These models offer minimal evidence of a dynamic autoregressor coefficient,
validating the use of static models which restrict effects to the contemporaneous period.
\par
Throughout this analysis, I prioritise extensions which expose the econometric properties of the model,
establish a grounding for future analysis.
I propose that this baseline establishing an investigation method,
with credible contributions to the data science, theory, and econometric techniques of issue
are sufficient for the scope of this paper.
Policy-relevant extensions are discussed in Appendix \ref{ss:param-select-xvalidate},
which I leave it for further analysis to execute.
These have the potential to investigate the policy-meaningful questions I discuss in this introduction.

\subsection{Productivity in the United Kingdom}

The United Kingdom was a regional economic powerhouse through the Industrial Revolution.
From the concentration of textiles in Manchester
to car manufacturing in the West Midlands,
as the natural geographic distribution that leant itself to heavy industries,
so did national interest in regional economic policy.
Regional focus declined strongly in the UK under the 1980s Thatcher government
and was largely maintained at lows throughout the 1990s-2000s New Labour period,
where rapid economic growth known as `The Great Moderation' satiated impetus for change.
\par
The consensus was broken in 2017, with the seeds of a regional-industrial strategy in 2017,
emerging under the Conservative May government.
Under the background of a UK-EU Referendum result (``Brexit'')
which emphasised the sentiment of disaffection among `left-behind' places across the UK
which did not see the benefits of national aggregates of economic growth
primarily concentrated in the UK's capital, London.
The national government launched `Local Industrial Strategies' which sought to
identify action to "boost productivity, earning power, and competitiveness" on a regional
basis\footnote{\cite{Mahmood2025}}.
However, fewer of these strategies were fully developed and implemented by 2021.
\par
A revival of interest in regional, pro-second city policy
has also taken place in the policies of the 2024 and 2026 governments in the United Kingdom.
In 2026, the newly appointed Burnham government made the pledge for ``good growth in every
postcode''\footnote{\cite{GOVUK2026}} with immediate speculation and interpretation from
economic commentators\footnote{\cite{Gill2026}, \cite{DeSilva2026}} on the details for
the direction of regional and urban policy in the United Kingdom in the absence of further details.
\par
It is well understood through the literature that follows that high productivity tends to follow urban clusters,
and specialisation and devolution can be an engine for economic growth if deployed appropriately.
However, the evidence on that appropriate manner is muddied at best,
with most political and economic insight limited to observing and recounting trends of recent cluster development.
There have been modern success stories in the United Kingdom, such as the sudden and successful
rise of Canary Wharf in London as a financial hub, the emerging life sciences cluster in Cambridge
and the `Golden Triangle', and creative industries such as video-game development in Edinburgh and the North of England.
Nevertheless, with the UK government pushing to go further and deploy substantial fiscal tools
as a means of regional productivity shocks\footnote{\cite{HMTreasury2026}},
the crucial policy question remains on the endogenous sustainability of a spatially-connected system.
Can the rise in productivity associated with clusters be attributed internally to the cluster itself,
or is the result of repeated, other external factors? Guidance on this is crucial for effective policy.
This analysis seeks to establish a baseline from which that evidence can be generated.
