\section{Introduction}
\label{s:intro}

The strength of the spatial productivity spillover
is crucial to policy and economic discussions on growth, production,
and the formation of urban clusters, but it is notoriously tricky to
estimate\footnote{Qualitative discussions in \cite{Krugman1998}, \cite{Fujita2004}}.
The purpose of this paper is to demonstrate that modern advances in
rich microdata compilation, computing, and econometric theory and techniques
mean that progress is possible in this decades-long empirical problem.
Indeed, evidence is derived with a stylised, classical model
only using features available from firm data,
which provides a baseline for future more sophisticated models of households,
urban development, and endogenous selection.
This analysis offers four key contributions that are substantively novel:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The development of data science techniques to calculate spatial transformations
            on rich microdata to fulfil exogenous regression specifications
    \item The application of higher-order spatial lags as an internal instrument
            deploying a peer effects model in a firm productivity setting
    \item The application of higher-order temporal lags as an internal instrument
            in a dynamic panel peer effects model,
            contrasting model performance with these two instruments
    \item Empirical estimates for a firm-to-firm spatial productivity spillover.
    This is evidence that can underlie patterns of firm colocation discussed
    in the more sophisticated urban development literature,
    and provides a framework for future extensions to investigate heterogenous effects.
\end{enumerate}
\par % Section 2: literature
This analysis acknowledges a large body of work on agglomeration,
rooted historically in \cite{Marshall1920}, which elucidate the importance of
urban density and development on firm productivity.
In section 2, I give an overview of the substantive contributions from
urban economics and New Economic Geography (NEG),
which explain agglomeration driver channels
and offer sophisticated approaches to modelling to household and firm dynamic location choices.
No attempt is made to recreate this whole body of work.
Instead, this analysis abstracts away from the usual components of urban density
and dynamic decision models. Despite being a spatial analysis,
location is barely employed as a feature in the analysis of productivity
except to identify each firm's spatial peers.
From this mere spatial proximity,
this analysis looks for empirical evidence isolating the pure productivity spillover
from peers to a target firm.
It therefore may provide empirical support for the centrifugal, pro-urbanisation force
underlying the more sophisticated analyses of New Economic Geography and regional development.
\par
Section 2 also gives a brief overview of the econometric literature
on peer effects model, from \cite{Manski1993}'s `Reflection Problem'
to finding valid instruments in a more complex graph and network setting in \cite{Bramoulle2009}.
This analysis leans heavily on that econometric theory
to develop a model of peer effects for the productivity spillover between firms.
\par
In sections 3 and 4, I discuss the handling of data from Fame,
a database of publicly-available, balance-sheet data from UK companies,
unified and designed to be comprehensive for research.
The cleaned Fame dataset provides more than 100,000 firms' data
across many years.
This is extremely useful for deriving precise econometric estimates,
but a significant data science challenge to compute
and manage the memory for vectors of millions of observations and their transformations.
In these sections, I highlight my use of data science techniques
to calculate the spatial weighting matrix $\mathbf W$
and the iterated evaluation of its higher-order spatial lags of $\mathbf W$,
such as $\mathbf W^2, \mathbf W^k, (\mathbb I-\mathbf W)$,
on over 1 million observations.
This offers a reusable method to calculate valid instruments
and conduct transformations that are helpful for eliminating endogeneity.
I provide a detailed guide to reproducing data methods in Appendix \ref{app:data-cleaning}
and all code for this analysis is available publicly\footnote{https://github.com/aloysiuslip/ucl-diss-ECON0079}.
\par
In section 5, I give the econometric analysis of a static model with spatial peer effects,
conceptualisable as `spatial lags' of the classical regressors in a 3-factor production function.
This implements econometric theory from \cite{Bramoulle2009} for the
identification of higher-order spatial lags as valid instruments to the \itx{endogenous} regressor.
I estimate models under both a `groups' peer structure,
which argues that the strength of a productivity spillover
is the average of nearby neighbours, and a `networks' model,
which applies an individualised weight for each target-peer firm connection
proportionate to the reciprocal of the physical distance between them.
\par
In section 6, I expand the use of internal instruments to higher-order temporal lags.
This builds on modern `System GMM' methods from \cite{Blundell1998}.
These include an autoregressor and exploit the time variation of the panel structure
to instrument for both the \itx{endogenous} peer effect components
and all other regressors, which have their assumptions relaxed to weak endogeneity.
These models offer limited evidence of a dynamic autoregressor coefficient,
or significant coefficients for lagged peer effect variables,
validating the use of static models which restrict effects to the contemporaneous period.
