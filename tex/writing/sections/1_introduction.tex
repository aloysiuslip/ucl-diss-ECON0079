\section{Introduction}

% Section 2: literature
Estimating the strength of a spatial productivity spillover
is crucial to policy and economic discussions on growth, production,
and the formation of urban clusters.
The purpose of this paper is to demonstrate that modern advances in
rich microdata collection, computing, econometric techniques, and economic theory
mean that it is possible to make significant progress in the decades-long
empirical problem of estimating spatial spillovers from a stylised, classical model,
even with a minimally sophisticated model of urban development
and the selection of firms to their location.
This allows us to provide underlying evidence for a spatial productivity spillover
between firms that drives the motivation for colocation, urban development,
and ultimately regional economic policy.
\par
This analysis sits on top of a large body of work,
rooted historically in \cite{Marshall1920} on agglomeration,
elucidating the importance of colocation and urban development on firm productivity.
In section 2, I give an overview of the substantive contributions from
spatial and urban economics and New Economic Geography (NEG)
to our body of knowledge behind the theoretical driver channels behind agglomeration,
and the sophisticated approaches to modelling to household and firm dynamic location choices.
This analysis does not attempt to recreate that whole body of work.
It abstracts away from the usual components of urban density,
stylised decision-making about the siting decision of household and firms,
and accessibility of urban resources such as transport accessibility.
Indeed, despite being a spatial production analysis,
location is barely a feature in the analysis of firm productivity
except as the component to draw each firm on a (2-dimensional, curved to the True Earth map)
network graph and find its nearby peers.
From mere spatial proximity,
this analysis looks for evidence of a productivity spillover
from peers to a target firm.
It deploys a simple classical production function model of only firms
with minimal modification to include a spatial spillover component.
A productivity spillover, as defined as an input to production,
can be conceptualised as a pure supply-side effect.
The above-discussed literature notes it as notoriously
tricky\footnote{Qualitative discussions in \cite{Krugman1998}, \cite{Fujita2004}}
to isolate this effect from demand, colocation effects, and third-party factors of urbanisation
such as the additional supply of public capital via infrastructure.
This analysis' empirical evidence isolating the pure spillover effect occurring on
a firm's Total Factor Productivity may provide further empirical support
for the centrifugal, pro-urbanisation force underlying the more sophisticated analyses
on New Economic Geography and policy discussions of agglomeration.
\par
Section 2 also gives a brief overview of the econometric literature
on peer effects model, from \cite{Manski1993}'s `Reflection Problem'
to finding valid instruments in a more complex graph and network setting in \cite{Bramoulle2009}.
This analysis leans heavily on that econometric theory
to develop a model of peer effects for the productivity spillover between firms.
\par
In sections 3 and 4, I discuss the handling of data from Fame,
a database of publicly-available, balance-sheet data from UK companies,
unified and designed to be comprehensive for research.
Fame offers the realisation of 100,000's of firms' data across
over 1 million firm-yearly observations.
This is a blessing for robust econometric analysis,
but a curse for scalable, recreatable data handling to handle both
the computation of millions of datapoints and their transformations,
and safely manage its memory flow.
In particular, I highlight two significant data challenges for this setting:
the architecture design around spatial data, including calculating distance-to-distance peers
and forming the spatial weighting matrix $\mathbf W$,
and the iterated application and evaluation of high indices of $\mathbf W$,
ex: $\mathbf W^2, \mathbf W^k, (\mathbb I-\mathbf W)$
as both transformations in reduced form and to calculate valid instruments.
The use of a high-performance, column vector-based data system, \itx{DuckDB}
was critical in this analysis for these purposes.
All code for this analysis is available publicly\footnote{https://github.com/aloysiuslip/ucl-diss-ECON0079}.
\par
In section 5, I give the econometric analysis of a static model with spatial peer effects,
conceptualisable as `spatial lags' of the classical regressors in a 3-factor production function.
This is a complex materialisation of \cite{Manski1993}'s `Reflection Problem',
meaning the identification of \itx{endogenous} regressors
requires careful treatment in a peer effects setting.
I show that modern econometric theory in a network setting, as given in \cite{Bramoulle2009},
can cleanly and intuitively apply to this firm productivity spillover problem.
I estimate a reduced-form peer effects model under both a `groups' peer structure,
which argues that the strength of a productivity spillover
is the average of nearby neighbours, and a `networks' model,
which applies an individualised weight for each target-peer firm connection
proportionate to the reciprocal of the physical distance between them.

\par
In section 6, I expand the use of internal instruments to use deep lags to identify
exogenous variation in the otherwise-endogenous peer effects regressors.
This builds on modern `System GMM' methods from \cite{Blundell1998}.
These are popular approaches to efficiently estimate coefficients
from endogenous variables in a dynamic system exploiting the time variation of the panel structure.
These models are likely the most robust given in this analysis.
They suggest a more limited effect of peer-target productivity spillover.
All specifications so a zero effect of a lagged TFP-spillover,
and autoregressive term with a very narrow confidence interval,
suggesting a robust result that if any productivity spillover does occur between firms,
it happens in the contemporaneous period.

%% Background policy
A revival of interest in regional, pro-second city policy
has also taken place in the policies of the 2024 and 2026 governments in the United Kingdom.
Regional economic policy declined strongly in the UK under the 1980s Thatcher government\dots
This consensus was maintained throughout the 1990s-2000s New Labour period,
where rapid economic growth known as `The Great Moderation' satiated impetus for change.
The consensus was broken in 2017, with the seeds of a regional-industrial strategy in 2017,
emerging under the Conservative May government.
Under the background of a UK-EU Referendum result (``Brexit'')
which emphasised the sentiment of disaffection among `left-behind' places across the UK
which did not seen the benefits of national aggregates of economic growth
primarily concentrated in the UK's capital, London.
The national government launched `Local Industrial Strategies' which sought to
identify action to "boost productivity, earning power, and competitiveness" on a regional
basis\footnote{\cite{Mahmood2025}}.
However, fewer of these strategies were fully developed and implemented by 2021.
\par
In 2026, the newly appointed Burnham government made the pledge for ``good growth in every
postcode''\footnote{\cite{GOVUK2026}} with immediate speculation and interpretation from
economic commentators\footnote{\cite{Gill2026}, \cite{DeSilva2026}} on what this might mean
for the direction of regional and urban policy in the United Kingdom in the absence of further details.
