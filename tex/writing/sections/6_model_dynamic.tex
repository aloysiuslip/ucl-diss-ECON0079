\section{Dynamic models}
\label{s:models-dynamic}

The previous section's static models requires a strict factor exogeneity assumption
outlined in equation \eqref{eq:static-identification-factor-exogeneity}
for all firms, including the own-firm effect of the production function.
In this section, I use a dynamic model to relax this assumption.
I also introduce a vector of temporal regressors:
an autoregressor of own-firm GVA as a component of its TFP,
and allowing for the past peer TFP components I analysed
in the static model to influence current-period TFP
in a lagged fashion.
The structure of this specification now resembles a vector panel,
which I will denote with VAR(p,q)\footnote{$q$-lags refers
to those on the peer spillover effect, rather than those of a moving average component
as is traditional of a VARMA(p,q) specification}.
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:dyn-main-reduced}
    z_{i,t} &= \alpha_i + \gamma_t + \beta_0 + \sum_{k=1}^p\phi_k y_{i,t-k}
        + \sum_{k=0}^q\mathbf z_{t-k}^{spill}\boldsymbol\theta_k + \varepsilon_{i,t}
    \\
    \label{eq:dyn-main-peer-effects}
    y_{i,t} &= \alpha_i + \gamma_t + \beta_0 + \beta_1 k_{i,t} + \beta_2 l_{i,t} 
                + \sum_{k=1}^p\phi_k y_{i,t-k} + \varepsilon_{i,t}
                \\ \notag &\qquad 
                + \sum_{k=0}^q \left[
                    \delta_k \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij}y_{j,t}
                    + (\xi_k-\delta_k\beta_1) \cdot \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij}k_{j,t}
                    + (\zeta_k-\delta_k\beta_2) \cdot \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij}l_{j,t}
                \right]
\end{align}

Adding the autoregressor $y_{i,t}$ is highly desirable.
The panel setup with firm fixed effects already accounts for own-firm factors,
and the presentation of $R^2$ including and excluding variation accounted for by the fixed effects
throughout this paper demonstrates this.
In the peer effects setting, autoregressive lags may also help control for reverse causality.
If $y_{i,t-1}$ is causal for $z_{jt}$,
or feasibly $z_{j,t-1}$ and $\mathbb E[z_{j,t-1}, z_{j,t}]\neq0$,
then the inclusion of $y_{i,t-k}$ eliminates that bias.
I offer no \itx{a priori} view on the strength of $\phi_1$, the autoregressor coefficient.
A significant estimate would imply heavy bias to the static models, validating the use in this dynamic setting.
\par
There are also significant theoretical advantages to introducing the $q$-lags of the spatial spillover.
This specification with both spatial and temporal lags accounts for the possibility
that the spillover effect from firm $j$'s production factor
has a lagged impact on $i$, conceptualisable as the formation of clusters
from firms seeing the benefits of colocation over some time after a productivity shock.
Without including the $q$ lags within $z_{i,t}$,
if $\mathbf z_{t-k}^\text{spill}$ is correlated with $\mathbf z_{t}$,
then estimating $\delta_0$ in a static model when the true effect is dynamic
exposes estimation to omitted variable bias (OVB) by attribution of the lagged to the current period's regressor.
\par
In practice for this dataset however, it is unlikely that more-than-one period spillover lag is relevant.
This is due to this analysis using Fame's annual frequency observations.
As a result, I show the structural equations in an AR(1,0) structure
so that the primary advancement on the static models
are purely the use of the weak exogeneity assumption, and first-order autoregressor.
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:dyn-main-ar10}
    y_{i,t} &= \alpha_i + \gamma_t + \beta_0 + \beta_1 k_{i,t} + \beta_2 l_{i,t} 
                + \phi_1 y_{i,t-k} + \varepsilon_{i,t}
                \\ \notag &\qquad 
                + \delta_k \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij}y_{j,t}
                + (\xi_0-\delta_0\beta_1) \cdot \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij}k_{j,t}
                + (\zeta_0-\delta_0\beta_2) \cdot \sum_{j\neq i} w_{ij}l_{j,t}
\end{align}

Estimation of this model is near-identical in reduced form
to the coefficients described in the static problem,
requiring only that I estimate one additional parameter $\hat\phi_1$.
Instead, the crucial change comes in the choice of instruments.
The dynamic specification allows the investigation to
employ and contrast the use
of temporal higher-order lags, rather than spatial ones.

\subsection{Dynamic panel identification}

The dynamic panel specification uses a autocorrelation error orthogonality assumption
to find valid identification \eqref{eq:dyn-no-ac}.
This replaces \eqref{eq:static-identification-factor-exogeneity} and instead allows
for some moderate level of cross-sectional error correlation \eqref{eq:dyn-xs-corr-errors}.
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:dyn-no-ac}
    \mathbb E[\varepsilon_{i,t}\varepsilon_{i,t-1}] &= 0       ,\qquad \forall t,t-1
    \\
    \label{eq:dyn-xs-corr-errors}
    \mathbb E[\varepsilon_{i,t}\varepsilon_{j,t}] &\neq 0   ,\qquad \exists i,j
    \\
    \label{eq:dyn-error-wn}
    \mathbb E[\varepsilon_t]    =0,
    \mathbb E[\varepsilon_t^2]  =\sigma_t^2,
    \mathbb E[\varepsilon_t|\Omega_{t-1}] &=0                  ,\qquad \forall i
    \qquad\text{(w.n.)}
\end{align}

No autocorrelation of errors may generally be seen as a more credible assumption
than strictly no cross-correlation.
In a dynamic, correctly-specified model with the correct autoregressive properties,
time autocorrelation as component of the error term are modelled out.
Conceptually, we could also argue that firms make efficient decisions,
so if there is a shock observed in this period, firms ensure that in the next,
any expected factor inputs that are realised by that period fully take advantage of the shock
and its effect is then captured by the input regressors.
Any remaining residual must have been expected and so mean zero (formally a Martingale sequence).
Further econometric analyses could guarantee this assumption by plotting patterns of errors
and check for autocorrelation, which could come from moving average
or omitted latent variable structures to the specification missing here.
This specification can be identified in reduced panel form,
which requires first differences to eliminate fixed effects,
allowing me to retain the use of firm fixed effects.
\par
Estimation under these dynamic panel conditions are developed by \cite{Anderson1981},
with further techniques expanded in \cite{Arellano1991} and \cite{Blundell1998} which improve efficiency.
The estimator given in \cite{Arellano1991} is designed to allow for
endogeneity, limiting only to weak exogeneity as I do here.
Internal instruments of the endogenous regressors are found using their lagged first differences of the log variables.
Identification is improved in the System GMM approach given in \cite{Blundell1998}
by also including the first logged, not differenced, lag of the endogenous regressors
which meets the usual instrument conditions, as an instrument.
I estimate this section's results with
System GMM\footnote{Implemented with \cite{Wu2023}, with heavy modifications in \cite{Lip2026}}.
Valid instruments are therefore theoretically derived as $\Delta x_{i,\tau-j}$ for any $j$
if $x_{i,\tau}$ is a valid instrument.

\subsection{Exploring temporal lag structures}
\label{ss:dyn-ar}

I firstly relax the strict exogeneity assumption of the standard factors to weak exogeneity.
There are two approaches to this:
capital and labour can be assumed to be predetermined:
capital faces some time-to-build constraint
where each firm allocates investment in the previous period
based on the limited information available then, $\Omega_{t-1}$.
The predetermined nature of capital is a common modelling choice of
firm production in a dynamic system, though usually with quarterly observations.
A similar sticky decision pattern is assumed of labour,
perhaps through job searching and hiring frictions.
For handling panel data in an autoregressive setting,
first-differencing will lead to the reduced-form error term $\Delta\varepsilon_t$
spanning 2 periods.
As a result, weak exogeneity of the transformed factors
despite the factors being predetermined in levels in period $t$
requires lags of $t-1$.
These lags are valid instruments with defensible exclusion restrictions.
Since the zero autocorrelation property applies to any firm $i$,
the full vector of deep lag factor regressors and their transformations
are plausibly exogenous.
\begin{align}
    \notag
    \text{Pre-determined factors:}
    \\ \notag
    K_{i,t} &= I_{i,t} + dK_{i,t-1}
    \\ \notag
    I_{i,t} &= \mathbb E[I_{i,t}^*|\Omega_{t-1}]
    \\ \notag
    L_{i,t} &= \mathbb E[L_{i,t}^*|\Omega_{t-1}]
    \\ \notag
    K_{i,t}, L_{i,t} &\in \Omega_{t-1}
    \\ \notag
    \mathbb E[\varepsilon_{i,t}|\Omega_{t-1}] &= 0 
    \\
    \label{eq:dyn-weak-exog-pred}
    \mathbb E[
        \varepsilon_{i,t}
        \vert
        \begin{pmatrix}
            k_{i,\tau} & l_{\tau}
        \end{pmatrix}
    ] &= 0_{(1\times t)}
    ,\ \forall i\ \forall \tau\leq t
    \\
    \label{eq:dyn-weak-exog-pred-diff}
    \mathbb E[
        \Delta\varepsilon_{i,t}
        \vert
        \begin{pmatrix}
            \Delta \mathbf W^k\mathbf k_{\tau-1} & \Delta \mathbf W^k \mathbf l_{\tau-1}
        \end{pmatrix}
    ] &= 0_{(1\times t)}
    ,\ \forall k\ \forall \tau\leq t
\end{align}

Alternatively, capital and labour may be flexible inputs:
at least partially able to react to shocks
as part of the decision set at time $t$.
This may be a more realistic assumption when handling annual data, such as Fame:
shocks occurring at the beginning of a calendar year would have no arguable
reason why the firm's input choices later in that year hadn't reacted to knowledge of the shock,
any more than it had occurred at the end of the previous year.
It is therefore much harder to argue the plausibly exogeneity of contemporaneous factors to the error term.
In practice, as with any dynamic panel model, the procedure laid out in this paper
is not bound by the features of this particular dataset, which happens to be annual.
Future analysis repeating this question with quarterly observations may well
exploit the plausible predetermined nature of some production factors.
In these cases, exogeneity in the reduced form first-differenced model
require deep lags of $t-2$ for exogeneity.
\begin{align}
    \notag
    \text{Flexible factors:}
    \\ \notag
    K_{i,t}, L_{i,t} &\in \Omega_{t}
    \\ \notag
    \mathbb E[\varepsilon_{i,t}|\Omega_{t-1}] &= 0 
    \\
    \label{eq:dyn-weak-exog-flex}
    \mathbb E[
        \varepsilon_{i,t}
        \vert
        \begin{pmatrix}
            k_{i,\tau-1} & l_{\tau-1}
        \end{pmatrix}
    ] &= 0_{(1\times t)}
    ,\ \forall i\ \forall \tau\leq t
    \\
    \label{eq:dyn-weak-exog-flex-diff}
    \mathbb E[
        \Delta\varepsilon_{i,t}
        \vert
        \begin{pmatrix}
            \Delta \mathbf W^k\mathbf k_{\tau-2} & \Delta \mathbf W^k \mathbf l_{\tau-2}
        \end{pmatrix}
    ] &= 0_{(1\times t)}
    ,\ \forall k\ \forall \tau\leq t
\end{align}
\par
It is certain that the autoregressor, $y_{t-1}$ is endogenous.
$y_{t-1}$ mechanically contains a $\varepsilon_{t-1}$ residual term;
first differencing for the reduced-form panel means that $\Delta\varepsilon_t$
and $\Delta y_t$ will contain a linear combination of $\varepsilon_t$ and $\varepsilon_{t-1}$.
When $\Delta y_{t-1}$ contains a linear combination of $\varepsilon_{t-1}$ and $\varepsilon_{t-2}$,
they share a common component, violating exogeneity.
Here, a simple first-order lag is sufficient temporal distance to give an instrument with exclusion.
\begin{align}
    \Delta y_{i,t-2} &\propto \Delta \varepsilon_{i,t-2} \Delta \varepsilon{i,t-3}
    \\
    \mathbb E[\Delta \epsilon_{i,t}\vert \begin{pmatrix}
        \Delta \varepsilon_{i,t-2} & \Delta \varepsilon{i,t-3}
    \end{pmatrix}] &= 0_{1\times 2}
\end{align}
This is additionally confirmed by Anderson-Rubin (AR) tests across all specifications,
which testing the autoregressor $\Delta y_{t-1}$,
consistently reject the null hypothesis at 1\% significance.
By contrast, the AR(2) test, which checks for the same weak instruments
but at the second-order lag $\Delta y_{t-2}$,
there is repeatedly insufficient evidence to reject the null hypothesis,
again confirming the theory of the System GMM that deep lags are a valid instrument.
With coefficients estimated across such a vast and heterogenous dataset,
these consistent results for the basic properties are useful to observe.
\par
As the properties of the spillover regressor, $\mathbf z^\text{spill}$ are unknown \itx{a priori},
I employ a $t-2$ deep lag at minimum to instrument for that component.
This leaves only the question about the classical regressors as discussed.
\par
In this dataset, the empirical experiments run suggest that the decision
over whether the classical factors should be predetermined or not
make no difference.

Across all specifications, the test-statistic for the Sargan-Hansen J-test

can be met, requiring any change to the predetermined assumptions.
This offers 2 pathways: either I can use the results of this test with confidence
and proceed with the assumption of capital and labour as predetermined variables.
This offers an additional time-axis observation, and thereby degree of freedom,
to conduct this panel analysis.
Alternatively, I can be cautious and continue to employ deep lags of at least $t-2$
for capital and labour instruments.
I proceed with the latter for this section's exposition,
owing to the richness of Fame in the time dimension.
Data samples with alternative cuts, such as subsections of the data examining
a certain time, industry, or region, may justify the alternative option at this stage.

\subsection{Higher-order instruments}
\label{ss:dyn-instr-ho}

A decomposition of the spatially endogenous component of the regression, $\mathbf Wy_t$,
has a similar form to the static problem.
It is helpful to show this to aid the construction of valid instruments:
it is obvious writing $y_{j,t}$ in reduced form in \eqref{eq:dyn-reduced-endog-y}
from where an exogenous instrument might come.
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{y}_t 
    &= \boldsymbol{\alpha} 
        + \gamma_t \boldsymbol{\iota} 
        + \beta_0 \boldsymbol{\iota} 
        + \beta_1 \mathbf{k}_t 
        + \beta_2 \mathbf{l}_t 
        + \phi_1 \mathbf{y}_{t-1} 
    \\ &\qquad \notag
        + \delta_0 \mathbf{W} \mathbf{y}_t
        + (\xi_0 - \delta_0\beta_1) \mathbf{W}\mathbf{k}_t 
        + (\zeta_0 - \delta_0\beta_2) \mathbf{W}\mathbf{l}_t
        + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t
    \\[1em]
    \label{eq:dyn-reduced-spatial-lag}
    &= \sum_{k=0}^\infty \delta_0^k \mathbf{W}^k \big( 
        \boldsymbol{\alpha} 
        + \gamma_t \boldsymbol{\iota} 
        + \beta_0 \boldsymbol{\iota} 
        + \beta_1 \mathbf{k}_t 
        + \beta_2 \mathbf{l}_t \notag
        + \phi_1 \mathbf{y}_{t-1} 
    \\ &\qquad \notag
        + (\xi_0 - \delta_0\beta_1) \mathbf{W}\mathbf{k}_t 
        + (\zeta_0 - \delta_0\beta_2) \mathbf{W}\mathbf{l}_t
        + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t 
    \big)
    \\
    y_{j,t} 
    &= \dots 
        + \underbrace{
            \phi_1 y_{j,t-1}
        }_{\text{untransformed lag}_j}
        + \underbrace{ 
            \sum_{k=0}^\infty \left[ \delta_0^k \mathbf{W}^{k+1} \right]_{(j,)} 
            \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{k}_t & \mathbf{l}_t\end{pmatrix} 
            \begin{pmatrix} \xi_0 \\ \zeta_0 \end{pmatrix} 
        }_{\text{contemporaneous exogenous}_j}
    \\ &\qquad \notag
        + \underbrace{ 
            \sum_{k=0}^\infty \left[ \phi_1 \delta_0^k \mathbf{W}^k \right]_{(j,)} 
            \mathbf{y}_{t-1} 
        }_{\text{lagged spatial}_j}
        + \underbrace{ 
            \sum_{k=0}^\infty [\delta_0^k \mathbf{W}^k]_{(j,)} 
            \cdot \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t 
        }_{\text{error transformation}_j}
\end{align}
The comparison to equation \eqref{eq:static-red-endogenous-effect} is evident:
the contemporaneous spatial lag term is still present,
meaning $[\mathbf W^k]_{j,} \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf k_t & \mathbf l_t\end{pmatrix}$
is still a valid instrument.
However, this may not be so useful under a cautious approach
allowing for cross-sectional non-orthogonality of errors \eqref{eq:dyn-xs-corr-errors},
as $\mathbf W_{j,} \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf k_t & \mathbf l_t\end{pmatrix}$,
the spatial \itx{exogenous effect} regressor is itself endogenous, or only weakly exogenous,
so higher-order spatial and temporal lags should be used to instrument for this term already.
\par
This decomposition yields several over new exogenous terms due to the lags involved.
The first is the simple untransformed lag component, $\phi_1 y_{j,t-1}$,
which still persists in the reduced form even after the spatial lag inversion.
This permits the use of simple deep lags, $y_{j,t-2}, y_{j,t-3},\dots$ as instruments.
There is an additional combined temporal and spatial lag transformation component,
$[\mathbf W^k]_{j,}y_{t-1}$.
This derivation shows that there are up to 3 functionally unique variable transformations
which can be used as a valid instrument in this analysis:
I estimate the equation fulfilling the vector of moment conditions,
(\ref{eq:dyn-moment-conditions}) via GMM.
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:dyn-moment-conditions}
    \mathbb E[
        \Delta\varepsilon_{it}
        \vert
        \begin{pmatrix}
            \Delta y_{j,t-2}                    \\
            \Delta [\mathbf W^k]_{(j,)}y_{t-\tau}    \\
            \Delta [\mathbf W^k]_{(j,)}k_{t-\tau}    \\
            \Delta [\mathbf W^k]_{(j,)}l_{t-\tau}
        \end{pmatrix}
    ]
    &= 0 \forall k \forall \tau\geq 2
\end{align}
\par
It is a function of this analysis' unique application of a dynamic panel GMM
to a peer effects model that offers this plethora of lags and instruments
to employ and credibly eliminate exogeneity from the peer effects \itx{endogenous}
regressor while gaining an impressive amount of variance.
\par
I firstly adopt the classic dynamic panel GMM strategy proposed by \cite{Anderson1981}
and simply use the deeper lags of $y_{j,t-2},\dots$ as instruments.
With a rich temporal dataset such as Fame,
multiple deep lags allows for overidentification, provided that additional information is being gained.
I leave it for future analysis to employ Information Criteria tests
to determine optimal number of lags in the simple autoregressor instrument.
In Table \ref{tab:dyn_instruments_str}, I simply estimate \eqref{eq:dyn-main-ar10}
varying the number of autoregressor lags from 3 lags between $t-2$ to $t-4$, to 5 lags between $t-2$ to $t-6$.

While these results don't offer any obvious new insight from parameter estimates,
they do confirm that continuing with the higher simple autoregressor lag strategy
does not leave the estimation over-identified, with a continuing failure to reject

\par
Due to the time constraints of this investigation,
I leave it for future analysis to continue the narrative and exploration here
to continue to test each type of instrument individually
and in combination with each other.
As I deployed in \nameref{s:models-static},
estimating the first-stage of the reduced-form equation with simple OLS
and comparing R$^2$ would offer insights on the strengths of instruments
employed to explain the variation in the endogenous regressors.
Additionally, as already discussed, Information Criteria tests would
also helpfully determine optimal lags for use in instruments.
My hypothesis is some combination of the untransformed autoregressive deep lag
and spatially-transformed lagged \itx{endogenous} regressor
would significantly improve identification.

\subsection{Dynamic empirical results}
\label{ss:dyn-empirical-results}

The empirical tests so far as given in \tablerefp{tab:dyn_basic_str}
and \tablerefp{tab:dyn_instruments_str} offer very little support
for the deployment of the dynamic structure,
with a consistently zero parameter on the autoregressor coefficient $\phi_1$.
Additionally, specifications run so far with AR(1,q) structures of lag
on the spatial components so far fail to find any significant
coefficients for $\boldsymbol \theta_1, \boldsymbol\theta_2,\dots$
as occurs in the static case.
Taken as face value, this offers some credibility to the use of
a static model to outline the problem.
Especially with annually-frequency panel data,
results form this section would argue that nearly all the variation
of interest is explainable by the contemporaneous
classical and spatial regressors employed in section \ref{s:models-static}.
\par
Taken seriously, this would suggest that spatial productivity spillovers are a static problem.
As a policy question, it implies that firms are rational, efficient actors
who take advantage or colocate in an area to take advantage of the contemporaneous
spillovers exhibited from their high-performing neighbours.
The dynamic formation of a cluster either therefore reflect
repeated, serially uncorrelated productivity shocks
in a region attributable to other factors than merely the spillover
between high-performing firms,
or that firms move to a cluster continually over time
for some other driver than chasing the highest spillover
to their TFP levels.
\par
Overall, this section provides a framework to continuing to test
the use of dynamic instruments which may improve the explanatory
power of lagged components of the dynamic specification,
reaffirms theory from \cite{Arellano1991} on the use of deep lags as valid instruments,
and further identifies interesting combinations of spatial and temporal lags
that could prove powerful relevance terms to improve identification.
