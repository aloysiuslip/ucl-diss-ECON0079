\section{Data analysis}
\label{s:data}

\subsection{Fame}

I use data from Fame for this investigation.
Fame is a comprehensive database of UK firms with  balance sheet variables,
which can be summed to useful aggregates
for economic analysis: GVA, capital, and labour inputs.
It is provided by Moody's Analytics, an international credit agency,
and collects its data from UK firms via survey\footnote{\cite{MoodysAnalytics2026}}.
Its international counterpart, Orbis, offers a similar service for international counterparts.
This analysis could therefore be extended to offer insights comparing the spillover effects
of UK firms to their international counterparts.
For this investigation, given the background discussion laid out,
the UK dataset is sufficiently comprehensive and policy-relevant.
\par
Over 100 GB of raw data were accessed and downloaded from Fame in February 2025
by Professor Lars Nesheim for \cite{Irastorza-Fadrique2026} and reused for this project.
Raw data were ingested to \itx{DuckDB}, an open-source, MIT-licensed relational (SQL)
database architecture\footnote{\cite{Raasveldt2026}} optimised for high-compute variable manipulation.
This is crucial for the applications of $(n\times n)$ matrix transformations
to a variable $n$-length column vector, as I detail heavily for calculating peer weights
and high-order transformations.
\par
Every firm is tagged with raw, textual location properties.
I construct a hierarchy of favourability to parse these, in order:
\itx{latitude/longitude}, \itx{full address}, \itx{address} (split over multiple fields),
and \itx{postcode} only.
I additionally favour data listed for the firm's \itx{primary trading address}
over its \itx{registered office address} (headquarters).
When only postcode data was available, I matched postcodes
to the ONS' postcode lookup database\footnote{\cite{ons2025:nspl}},
yielding a decimal longitude-latitude value for the centre of its area.
I compiled these heterogeneous features to 7 location identifier columns.
The first set of these indicate membership of 3 spatial location groups:
travel-to-work area (\itx{ttwa}) using the 2011
definitions\footnote{Travel-to-work area definitions from 2011 are still considered
the mostly reliable for current analytical research,
due to the pollution in travel and working patterns resulting from the COVID-19 pandemic during the 2021 Census.
Travel-to-work-area area coverages are mostly time-invariant; changes between 2001 and 2011
were reasonably limited, see: \cite{ons2015:ttwa}.},
the first-half of a UK postcode (\itx{pc4}), or a full UK postcode (\itx{pc8}).
The latter 2 are point distance decimals for longitude + latitude.
\itx{longitude\_decimal}, \itx{latitude\_decimal},
and 2 supporting columns indicating the data quality.
\par
The resulting time-invariant table has exactly 152,379 firms
with unique Companies House \itx{registered\_number} identifiers.

\subsection{Deriving panel aggregates}
\label{ss:data-panel-aggregates}

All financial data are deflated to real terms, using 2024 as the base year.
I use CPIH\footnote{\cite{ons2026:cpih}, CPIH is the ONS' preferred price index
for household consumption \cite{ons2026:cpih-technical}} to deflate \itx{renumeration\_employees}
and other income-related aggregates capturing household spending patterns.
I use the GDP deflator\footnote{\cite{ons2026:qna}} for all other balance sheet variables.
Financial data are in thousands of GBP (£'000s);
as I estimate log relationships exclusively, this has no effect on regression coefficients.
I compute these variables for the exactly 1,128,490 panel observations.
Firms have a mean of 7 years of observations per firm.
Further detail on the cleaning procedure and derivation of variables
is available in Appendix \ref{app-ss:financial-data}.
\par
Labour input is the number of employees reported on the FAME balance sheet per firm.
This dataset does not include firm-level hours worked per employee that would offer
granular productivity (GVA per hour worked) measures.
An estimation in this setting would require imputing the measure from industry aggregates,
which would likely confound the true firm-level signal.
\par
Capital is Fame's \itx{fixed\_total} variable, which represents the sum of tangibles, intangibles, and other investments
within the balance sheet, namely an extremely comprehensive measure of the firm's fixed capital.
The average capital-output ratio is 6x within FAME when filtering for positive values \tablerefp{tab:data-financial-ky},
which is moderately higher than macroeconomic aggregates typically suggest
likely due to accounting standard differences.

GVA is calculated as the sum of \itx{ebitda} and \itx{renumeration\_employees}.
\itx{renumeration\_employees} is the exact sum of \itx{wages}, \itx{social\_security\_costs},
and \itx{pensions\_costs} in the balance sheet.
\itx{ebitda} is in turn \itx{turnover} minus intermediate inputs and is
given in the Fame balance sheet for the vast majority of panel observations
in our dataset. It is also possible to reconstruct \itx{ebitda}
via other balance sheet metrics,
but these sub-fields are actually available for fewer observations than \itx{ebitda}.

\pagebreak
To help guide the choice of aggregate measures and understand the observation structure,
I conduct a benchmark 2-factor production estimation in panel structure
to help validate the choice of aggregates against known measurements.
\begin{align}
    \label{eqn:tfp2factor}
    y_{i,t} &= \alpha_i + \gamma_t + \beta_0
                + \beta_1 k_{i,t}
                + \beta_2 l_{i,t}
                + u_{i,t}
\end{align}
This specification does not yield consistent estimates when estimated with OLS,
according to the structural model I give in section \ref{ss:static-identification}.
$u_{i,t}$ is a non-structural error-residual correlated with $k_{i,t}$ and $l_{i,t}$.
Nevertheless, it is a helpful exploration tool to observe if derived coefficients
match macroeconomic estimates

Coefficients derived in \tablerefp{tab:tfp_combined} give a labour income share, $\hat\beta_2$,
exactly in line with ONS analysis of UK industry-level data
over the same time period, particularly \itx{corporate gross labour share} which is constructed
from a similar sample to Fame\footnote{\cite{ons2024:labour-share-uk}}.
\par
I explore specifications which omit fixed effects.
In Model (2.), the capital share of income jumps on the omission of firm fixed effects
as all within-firm variation that we would decompose as $\alpha_i\in \varepsilon_{i,t}$
is instead attributed to capital.
This gives us some insight that in this panel setting,
the measure of capital as a stock variable is reasonably consistent within a firm over time.
\par
When varying regressor measures, surprisingly,
running the regression with \itx{fixed\_total} as the capital measure instead of \itx{total\_assets}
almost entirely eliminates the capital share of income, or explanatory power of capital on variation in GVA.
Similar results are shown for a further decomposition of \itx{fixed\_total} to its asset components.
I leave it for future analysis why this dataset yields this phenomenon and
for the rest of the analysis, I use \itx{total\_assets} as the primary capital measure.

\subsection{Financial and location data limitations}
\label{ss:data-limitations}

Following data cleaning procedures discussed in \cite{Irastorza-Fadrique2026},
I removed duplicate entries, limited observations to those with more than 10 employees,
and observations with the required balance sheet properties,
\itx{renumeration\_employees} and \itx{ebitda} required to calculate firm GVA.
\par
Fame has rich microdata observations with granular detail about each firm in the UK,
but it is not designed for spatial analysis without advanced data science techniques,
improving even beyond the sophistication demonstrated here.
There is some miscoding in Fame's location data.
Despite it being my favoured \itx{amalgmated address} selection choice,
Fame's native \itx{longitude} and \itx{latitude} data contained some errors,
such as recording either one of those $0.000$ coordinates, swapping them,
or placing them outside the UK.
I employed several techniques to search for outliers.
One instances is calculating the centre-point of all firms sharing a postcode
and then filtering for firms further than a threshold:
I evaluated that 1.8\% of all firms in the cleaned sample
are more than 5km away from their postcode's centre-point.
Due to the time and data processing constraints of this analysis,
I dropped these firms and observations.
\par
Though I recorded the quality of location data,
I undertake no further analysis using it as its incorporation.
I discuss further uses in section \ref{ss:param-select-xvalidate}
to control and investigate the effects of miscoded location data
and heterogenous treatment effects.
These requires complex data science and Machine Learning approaches,
which I leave for future analysis.
\par
I amalgamate multisite firms to their primary trading address, as an assumption.
In the dataset prepared for \cite{Irastorza-Fadrique2026},
each firm is only tagged with their last-known registered address and primary trading address.
Fame includes further trading addresses for multisite firms available for download through their data portal,
however the only site-level datapoint provided is \itx{number\_of\_employees} per trading address,
with sparse and inconsistent coverage\footnote{\cite{MoodysAnalytics2026}, platform accessed August 2026.}.
This analysis simply takes financial aggregates listed for each firm
and assumed it to be located at the listed primary trading address.
There are clear flaws to this method: large retail firms, such as consumer retailer `Tesco',
have several hundred listed trading addresses on Fame,
and generate much less than the full amount of GVA
at their listed primary trading location in `Welwyn Garden City'.
Nevertheless, difficult algorithm allocation decisions,
or the pursuit of more sophisticated data sources
are required to address this issue,
as discussed in section \ref{ss:ext-alt-location}.
The amalgamation assumption provides ease of computation 
while contributing to attenuation bias of the estimated spillover parameters
due to imperfect peer assignment.
\par
Additionally, the location data that is available is time-invariant,
a characteristic of the \itx{fixed} table, rather than updated on a yearly basis
to reflect firm-movers.
This is a reflection of using a comprehensive dataset of financial datapoints,
that was not optimised for spatial analysis; instead Fame provides an in-built feature
suggesting peers via balance sheet and industry similarity, which I ignore for this analysis.
One interpretation of this analysis' use of these characteristics
is that each firm is over the entire 20-year sample with no movers.
This seems very unlikely: \fullref{ss:lit-neg} tells us to expect
the endogenous location of firm movement and colocation
to optimise for taking advantage of firm spillovers.
\par
Using the location characteristics as I do,
as a noisy signal of the firm's true location, has 2 econometric consequences.
Firstly, firm fixed effects in all spatial specifications become extremely important.
For all movers within the sample period, an endogenous firm location decision
can only be modelled as correlated with the spillover regressors of interest.
This justifies my use of fixed effects over random effects throughout this analysis.
Firm fixed effects are time invariant, and can additionally help to eliminate
some of the endogeneity induced by movers who undertook their move
just before the sample period, 2006-, to maximise their spillover effect.
\par
Secondly, I acknowledge some attenuation to the productivity spillover estimate.
Incorrect location data due to any of the above-discussed issues
causes the assignment of spatial peers to any target to be miscalculated,
and vice versa.
Peer firms who were close to the target firm during the firm-year observation
who subsequently moved away within-sample will have an extreme distance recorded,
and so be excluded from tagged address groups and truncated distance calculations.
Peer firms who are recorded as close to the target firm as they subsequently moved to the location.
This reduces down the strength of the spillover coefficient $\theta_0$ estimated.

\subsection{Location group classification}
\label{ss:data-w-groups}

Throughout this analysis, I use terminology of a productivity spillover occurring
from a group or average of \itx{peer} firms onto a \itx{target} firm.
The influence of the vector of all firms $j$ on the vector of all firms $i$
can be summarised in an $(n \times n)$ interactions matrix, $\mathbf W$.
A weight $w_{ij}\in\mathbf W\neq 0$ implies a firm-to-firm interaction,
namely some use of the peer firm's attributes in the regression to estimate the target firm's TFP.
\par
This interactions matrix is symmetric: any firm with a peer weight
calculated by the distance between the two firms naturally is a peer in return
with an identical distance and weight.
Extensions to this analysis could devise asymmetric relations,
for example, with bigger firms having more influence.
The interactions relationship is irreflexive - no target can be a peer of itself.
This means the diagonal elements of $w_{ii}\in \mathbf W=0$.
I summarise the properties for $\mathbf W$ in Table \ref{tab:location-set-properties},
with further discussion for $\mathbf W_g$ and $\mathbf W_d$ to follow.

        \label{tab:location-set-properties}

The definition of a group within $\mathbf W_g$ is intuitive:
peers interact in a fixed limit of nearby firms,
where each peer within the group is treated as homogenous, offering an equal weighted value,
here $1/(s_g-1)$.
The group matrix $\mathbf W_g$ is transitive: if firms A and B share a spatial group,
\itx{ttwa}, \itx{pc4}, or \itx{pc8},
then a firm C that is a peer of firm B must also be of firm A.
Apart from its irreflexivity, $W_g$ is block diagonal.
I construct initial weights by peers which share the spatial group \itx{pc8},
so each firm has peers of all the firms in its postcode,
each weighted with the reciprocal of the number of firms in the postcode (leaving-one-out).
\par
Formal rules for construction of the group matrix are summarised
below\footnote{Adapted from \cite{Bramoulle2009}}.
I illustrate an example $\mathbf W_{g^*}$, a $(7\times 7)$ set of interactions
between firms which have group classifiers of size $s_1=4$ and $s_2=3$ only in Figure \ref{fig:static-groups-W}.

\begin{align*}
    w_{ij}\in \mathbf W_{g^*} &=
        \begin{cases}
            0,              & \text{if}         \ i=j           \\
            \frac1{s_g-1},  & \text{else if}    \ (i,j)\in g    \\
            0,              & \text{otherwise}
        \end{cases}
    \\
    \mathbf \Gamma_{s_1} \in \mathbf W_{g^*}
    &=
    \{ i, j \in g \}, s_1: n_g
    \\
    \mathbf W_{g^*}
    &=
    \underset{(n\times n)}{
        \begin{pmatrix}
            \mathbf \Gamma_{s_1}    &   0                       &   \dots       \\
            0                       &   \mathbf \Gamma_{s_2}    &   \dots       \\
            \vdots                  &   \vdots                  &   \ddots
        \end{pmatrix}
    }
\end{align*}

I can extend this to more complicated set of group rules.
I calculate non-overlapping group weights $\mathbf W_g$ to $\mathbf W_{g1}, \mathbf W_{g2}, \mathbf W_{g3}$ at once,
which represent the successive concentric rings of \itx{pc8} (core), \itx{pc4}, and \itx{ttwa}.
The weighting rules for these multiple, non-overlapping groups is to zero the $i,j$ interaction in
$W_{gk}$ if a prior matrix in the series, $W_{gl},\ l<k$ has a non-zero entry in that cell.
I illustrate an example in Figure \ref{fig:static-groups-donut-matrices}.
These matrices are orthogonal.
\begin{align*}
        w_{ij}\in \mathbf{W}_{gk} &=
        \begin{cases}
            0,             & \text{if } i=j \\
            0,             & \text{if } w_{ij,l} \neq 0, \exists l<k \\
            \frac{1}{s_g-1}, & \text{else if } (i,j)\in g \\
            0,             & \text{otherwise}
        \end{cases}
\end{align*}

\subsection{Location distance calculations}
\label{ss:data-distance}

A network matrix is a more complex set of interactions where each individual $i$ has their own set
of peers by whom they are influenced.
In the general definition \citep{Bramoulle2009},
the influence of each individual's peer set is still the simple `leave-one-out' average
of their heterogenous peers, however, differing weights applied within the peer set are equally permissible.
I use variants on the reciprocal of the spatial distance between the two firms
to construct my weights in $\mathbf W_d$ in this analysis.
Intuitively, these distance reciprocal weights are pretty similar.
I explore 3 models just to demonstrate that findings are robust to small modifications in the weights,
and that differing weighting strategies are permissible.
A simple reciprocal weight is common to the gravity models used in the urban economics literature
discussed\footnote{\todo{Gravity}}.
A harsher penalty for distance, $\frac1{\delta^2}$, has been employed
by the New Economic Geography literature, emphasising a more crucial role
for close proximity\footnote{\todo{NEG}}.
\begin{align}
    w_{ijt}\in \mathbf \Omega_t &=
        \begin{cases}
            \frac1{d_{ij}},         &\text{(1) gravity}                     \\
            \frac1{d_{ij}^2}        &\text{(2) N.E.G.}                      \\
            \exp{-d_{ij}\cdot k}    &\text{(3) exponential decline}
        \end{cases}
\end{align}

Distances are calculated as the Haversine distance
between any 2 firms. This represents the straight-line, `as-the-crow-flies',
fastest path between the longitude and latitude pairs given,
which has a slight adjustment from a standard straight-line distance
due to the (great-circle) curvature of the Earth, for which the effects are minimal
due to the small distances calculated.
I show a histogram of distances calculated between firms in Figure \ref{fig:hist-distances-20km}.

To reduce computational complexity,
I disregard any possible matches which don't share a Travel-to-Work-Areas (\itx{ttwa}).
Not only are these firms more likely to be further apart,
eliminating the need to calculate distances between, say,
London and Glasgow,
the \itx{ttwa} boundaries also a useful proxy for the functional boundaries
of interacting economic agents, so it is a reasonable
restriction to limit the peer effects of firms across those borders.
Additionally, I disregard any spatial connection over a 5km limit,
saving on memory and storage. The threshold provides a plausible limit
for the practical boundaries of one firm's peer effects on another,
aligning helpfully with when contributions to a weighted average aggregate
start to become too infinitesimal to matter using a reciprocal function for weights.
\par
One final helpful property within the $\mathbf W_d$ matrix
is the definition of a network itself.
A network is the total set of individuals who have at least one interaction
within the network:
\begin{equation*}
    j \in N_d\ \iff \mathbf W_{ij}\neq 0,\ \exists i \in N_d
\end{equation*}
There can exist several networks within a $\mathbf W$ interactions matrix,
as certain individuals within a peer set may have these complex interactions,
but all inside the set have nothing with those outside.
Organising these networks in a $\mathbf W_d$ gives an outer block-diagonal structure,
though with far larger blocks than $\mathbf W_g$.
By disregarding all firm pairs which don't share a \itx{ttwa},
I set the limits of a network to the \itx{ttwa} level.
Networks offer further calculation opportunities for network fixed effects
as discussed in section \ref{ss:ext-nfe}.
\par
$\mathbf W_d$ is non-transitive.
Firm B, a peer of firm A within its 5km radius, can clearly have its own peers
that are within its own 5km radius that are beyond that radius for firm A.
This matrix resembles a sparse matrix with a `thick' diagonal,
rather than a matrix with distinct large blocks.

from the cleaned dataset in Figure \ref{fig:static-network-4x5-Wd}.

To calculate $\mathbf W_d$, I store distances $d_{ij}$ in a one-to-one relational table,
and compile rows on an efficient as-needed for calculations.
The distance table has 734 million target-peer pairs;
paradoxically, this implies both an incredibly
rich, high-identification, interactions table due to a mean of 4,820 peers per target firm
and also that the $\mathbf W_d$ matrix is mostly (over 96\%) sparse.
The \itx{ttwa} and 5km boundaries are ultimately an arbitrary choice;
stricter restrictions to create networks only within \itx{pc8} boundaries
would have resulted in a $\mathbf W_d$ matrix that is 99.8\% sparse.
While these could be considered reasonable restrictions,
they matter a lot to the econometrics as firms disqualified as peers are not merely
excluded from the weighted average calculations,
but considered valid candidates for instruments, as they are non-transitive peers-of-peers.
Errors in the specification of this parameter could lead to Incorrect
specification of the peer group of the target firm.
I discuss in section \ref{ss:param-select-xvalidate} how the most appropriate approach
would be to cross-validate these non-linear parameter selections
in a choice regression specification, minimising mean-squared-error (or adjusted R$^2$).

\subsection{Higher-order $\mathbf W$ transformations}
\label{ss:data-matrix-weights}

Intuitively, I take the product of these weighting matrices $\mathbf W$ to the aggregate variables vectors
\itx{gva}, capital \itx{total\_assets}, and labour \itx{employees}.
As will be shown in \fullref{s:models-static}, the procedure to generate valid instrumental variables
also requires me to apply multiples of this transformation matrix,
exponents $\mathbf W^2, \mathbf W^3, \mathbf W^4$, and as an extension,
the higher-order cross-transformation for group matrices,
$\mathbf W_{g1}\mathbf W_{g2}, \mathbf W_{g1}\mathbf W_{g3}$ and so on to the aggregates.
\par
I calculate these for my 3 aggregate variable vectors of interest.
Summary statistics offering a sense of the shape of transformed data
are available in Appendix \ref{app-ss:w-transformations}.
\par
As reviewed in \ref{ss:lit-peer-effects-metrics}, the key condition
for discovery of valid instruments in this peer effects context is the linear independence of $\mathbb I, \mathbf W, \mathbf W^2$.
With the additional step of application of network fixed effects (section \ref{eq:ext-lnfe-reduced}),
this is additionally evaluated with $\mathbf W^3$.
I can trivially say this is true, meeting the alternative sufficient conditions in \ref{tab:bdf_identification}
of $\geq 3$ group sizes for the group $\mathbf W$ matrices.
\par
My distance-decay weights, $\mathbf W_{d1}, \mathbf W_{d2}, \mathbf W_{d3}$
meet the strong alternative $s_d\geq 3$ condition, where $s_d$ is the \itx{diameter} of the network,
the maximal number of links connecting any target $i$ with another firm through a series of peers $j, k, \dots$.
In the analysis of this set of firms with distance radius limits,
the \itx{diameter} is large, potentially spanning the full spatial diameter of a \itx{ttwa}.
It is this mechanism design provides another justification, alongside computation,
for limiting the max length of a target-firm peer relation to 5km:
it vastly increases the probability of `overlapping groups', i.e. a high diameter network within a \itx{ttwa}.
The overlapping group network structure lends itself nicely to illustrations.
Figure \ref{fig:map-nolg-manc} selects a random firm $i$ from my sample,
which happens to be in Stockport, Greater Manchester, and draws the distances used to calculate metric $d1$
between this target and its peers. In typical network diagram fashion, a network with a considerable \itx{diameter}
is created by taking a subset of $i$'s peers, then successively a set of their peers, then successively some of those
and so on. 4 iterations in this diagram fails to clearly hit the \itx{ittwa} boundary,
on the south side, with more argument for this behaviour on the north side
where peer nodes randomly selected may be starting to bunch at an `invisible' border of the \itx{ttwa} boundary.
