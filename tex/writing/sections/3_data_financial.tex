\section{Financial data}

\subsection{Fame}

I have used data from Fame for this investigation.
Fame is a comprehensive set of financial datapoints from UK firms with useful variables for this analysis,
in particular, number of employees and balance sheet data from which analysis-relevant aggregates can be derived.
Fame is provided by Moody's Analytics, an international credit agency,
and collects its data from UK firms via survey\footnote{\cite{MoodysAnalytics2026}}.
\par
Its international counterpart, Orbis, offers a similar service for international counterparts.
This analysis could therefore be extended to offer insights comparing the spillover effects of UK firms to their international counterparts.
For this investigation, the UK dataset is comprehensive and the most policy-relevant for this investigation.

\subsection{\todo{Ingestion and cleaning}}

I wrote a series of Python scripts\footnote{https://github.com/aloysiuslip/ucl-diss-ECON0079}, organised in Jupyter notebooks to ingest this data and manipulate this data.
Data was stored in \itx{DuckDB}, an open-source, MIT-licensed relational (SQL) database architecture\footnote{\cite{Raasveldt2026}}.
It operates locally, meaning minimal lag from issuance to execution time
provided the operating machine is sufficiently high-compute.
It stores data in column vectors, which is a significant advantage
to manipulating whole variables.
A notable use-case is the application of $(n\times n)$ Matrix transformations
to a variable $n$-length column vector,
as is required to calculate distance-weighted averages.
Any SQL commands issued from the Python are compiled down to C,
making execution for column transformation operations extremely fast,
even for joining tables of several 100s of millions of rows\footnote{explanatory
numbers given in this section are rounded to 3 significant figures for ease},
as is calculated in this analysis for target-peer spatial distances.
I wrote database interaction commands using \itx{Ibis}\footnote{\cite{TheIbisProjectAuthors2026}},
an Apache-2.0 licensed Object-Relational Mapping (ORM) framework
which allows SQL commands to be written natively in Python,
minimising the likelihood of errors when issuing queries loops or changing variable names.
This architecture choice is extremely well-suited for mass data econometric analysis
and critical to solving the compute limitations of iterative large-$n$ dataset manipulations
required to calculate the valid instruments identified in section \ref{ss:static-identification}.
\par
Over 100Gb of raw data was accessed and donwloaded from Fame in February 2025
by Professor Lars Nesheim for \cite{Irastorza-Fadrique2026} and reused for this project.
Raw data are stored in a series of Excel files on a series of a financial balance sheet indicators,
including characteristics that are time-invariant for each firm, bucketed into $a1\_ID$ and $a5\_misc$ files,
and annual data published on a calendar-year basis,
bucketed into \itx{a2\_key\_finance}, \itx{a3\_assets}, and \itx{a4\_profits} tables.
\par
I traversed the file structure to build a dictionary to the filepaths organised in their subfolders,
which are named by the 2-digit industry SIC division,
and batch-imported data belonging to the firms in that industry.
Some industries have very many firms, requiring further batching of their files,
such as SIC division 47 (Retail Trade, 1090 files)
and 82 (Office administrative, office support and other business support activities,
1065 files)\footnote{\todo{ref: ONS}}.
I limited a batch to 200 files for stable memory handling,
and used Python's multithreaded asynchronous \itx{concurrent.futures} package
to efficiently compute the data flow.
In total, 12,800 files were processed across the 88 available SIC divisions.
Time-invariant firm characteristics were saved to a table named \itx{fixed},
annual firm data to a table named \itx{yearly} within \itx{DuckDB}.
\par
All firm data are tagged with a `registered number' property corresponding to a Companies House ID.
I deduplicated 896,000 \itx{fixed} firm entries using a `registered number' match,
of which the vast majority, 772,000 were exact duplicate entries,
and the remainder had some mismatch of 1 or more firm characteristics,
such as the listed company name, global operating owner,
and in rare cases, location.
Duplicate entries are a sustained feature of the Fame dataset,
worsened by Fame ingesting different snapshots of firm data
at different points of time, where fixed characteristics may not sync
across snapshots, or change over time. There may also be data ingestion errors,
meaning slight variations in names referring to the same entity,
and failures to update evolving factors such as `number of datapoints available' for a firm.
When duplicates occurred, I effectively randomly chose an entry to keep.
A more robust analysis could devise further data science methods
to review differences between entries, such an acceptable Levenshtein distance
between name entries, or creating a proceducre to store and separately treat
firms with multiple listed addresses.
Further data cleaning procedures with this exact dataset are discussed in \cite{Irastorza-Fadrique2026}.
Significant recommended procedures include
avoiding double-counting of firms through both their parent company and subsidiaries,
truncation of data to at earliest 2010 due to changes in reporting standads,
and reclassification of firm SIC codes using NLP on firm business activity descriptions.
\par
As the main analysis presented here only uses location data as the \itx{fixed} characteristic of analysis,
due to its time constraints, and due to the low number of duplicates
and other cleaning errors listed above relative to the size of the dataset,
I treat those errors as random effects and deem the random selection strategy suitable.
I similarly removed duplicate firm-year entries in the \itx{yearly} data,
and merged the two tables based on ensuring every \itx{fixed} firm identity
had a corresponding \itx{yearly} set of rows, and vice versa.
Due to the method of ingestion handling each raw data file industry
as a bulk addition of rows for efficiency,
rather than merging panel observation rows by firm-year identifier at the point of ingestion
which would be a significant computation hassle,
I adopted a `max-merge' column strategy, which means that any panel observation duplicates
where one row has `0s' and the next has a value, the value is kept.
This however means that if two observations have conflicting values for a firm-year observation
and it is a random assignment which of those two values is the `true' one,
the larger one is kept, which has the potential to bias all financial data entries upwards.
This is unlikely to be correlated with any of the variables of interest investigated in this analysis.
I further removed a small minority (<200) firm entries not based in the UK,
which can be identified through their `registered number' format.
In total, deduplication reduced the number of firms from 9,280,000 to 8,220,000,
and the number of panel observations from 44,900,000 to 42,900,000.
\par
I restrict the sample to observations with fewer than 10 employees,
and drop any observation from the \itx{yearly} data which doesn't include
both \itx{renumeration\_employees} and \itx{ebitda} which are required to calculate firm GVA.
This follows the recommendation of \cite{Irastorza-Fadrique2026}
as low-employee observations are more likely to be SMEs weakly-defined trading purposes,
or shell companies, that would mechanically yield an upwardly-biased TFP-residual calculation
with few employees for GVA.
However, doing this at the observation-level, rather than the firm-level, likely also could induce
a bias to the variables measured as then firms included the sample whose
employees temporarily dipped below the threshold but then returned above would have their years of
low employee numbers excluded.
There is fortunately a buffer of one year either side due to first-differencing in panel regressions.
Nevertheless, I adopted the former, filtering on the observation level.
This set of filters reduced the number of valid firms in this analysis to 152,000
with 1,130,000 panel observations.

\par
All data is deflated to real terms, using 2024 as the base year.
I used the GDP deflator for all balance sheet variables,
except for \itx{renumeration\_employees} and its composites for which I used CPIH,
as these are income-related aggregates with real spending power best captured by household purchasing baskets
\footnote{\cite{OfficeforNationalStatistics2026}}.
\par
All balance sheet data are in thousands ('000s) of GBP (£).
As we estimate log relationships exclusively, this has no effect on parameter interpretation.

\subsection{Deriving panel aggregates}

Labour input is taken from the number of employees reported on the FAME balance sheet per firm.
This dataset, like all others, does not include hours worked per employee on a firm-level that would offer a more
granular productivity (GVA per hour worked) measures. An estimation in this setting would likely have to impute the measure
from industry aggregates, which would likely confound the true firm-level signal.
\par
Capital is Fame's \texttt{fixed\_total} variable, which represents the sum of tangibles, intangibles, and other investments
within the balance sheet, namely an extremely comprehensive measure of the firm's fixed capital.
The average capital-output ratio is 6x within FAME when filtering for positive values \tablerefp{table:ky},
which is moderately higher than macroeconomic aggregates typically suggest
likely due to accounting standard differences.

GVA is calculated as the sum of \itx{ebitda} and \itx{renumeration\_employees}.
\itx{renumeration\_employees} is the exact sum of \itx{wages}, \itx{social\_security\_costs},
and \itx{pensions\_costs} in the balance sheet.
\itx{ebitda} is in turn \itx{turnover} minus intermediate inputs and is
given in the Fame balance sheet for the vast majority of panel observations
in our dataset. It is also possible to reconstruct \itx{ebitda} via the sum of
\itx{profit\_loss\_pretax}, \itx{interest\_paid}, and \itx{depreciation},
providing an alternative measure of GVA.
We can expect the difference between these two to be accounting differences\footnote{
    An OLS regression between the two, discarding 5\% outliers, has a coefficient of 0.91 with a 0.000 p-value.
    This means \itx{gva2} is slightly lower than \itx{gva1}, with that impact mostly driven by top-end outliers.
}
and additionally note that the alternate GVA measure also reduces panel observations from 1,085,520 to 618,588.
For these reasons, I do not report TFP calculations with this second GVA measure
which are regardless near-identical, though I resultingly refer to GVA throughout this paper as \itx{gva\_1}.
\par
To help guide the choice of capital and output (GVA) measure, I conduct a quick benchmark
2-factor production estimation to help validate the choice of aggregates against known measurements.
\begin{align}
    \label{eqn:tfp2factor}
    y_{i,t} &= \alpha_i + \gamma_t + \beta_0
                + \beta_1 k_{i,t}
                + \beta_2 l_{i,t}
                + u_{i,t}
\end{align}
This specification is not valid OLS specification by the structural model I give later in \ref{ss:static-identification}.
$u_{i,t}$ is a non-structural error-residual that is correlated with $k_{i,t}, l_{i,t}$.
The estimation is a simple panel approach with time and fixed effects, and a constant.
Regressors are given in log terms to approximate a structural production function similar to \ref{eq:static-production-3-factor}.

for the rest of the analysis, I use \itx{total\_assets} as the primary capital measure.
