\section{Financial data}

\subsection{Fame}

I have used data from Fame for this investigation.
Fame is a comprehensive database of UK firms with  balance sheet variables,
which can be summed to useful aggregates
for economic analysis: GVA, capital, and labour inputs.
It is provided by Moody's Analytics, an international credit agency,
and collects its data from UK firms via survey\footnote{\cite{MoodysAnalytics2026}}.
Its international counterpart, Orbis, offers a similar service for international counterparts.
This analysis could therefore be extended to offer insights comparing the spillover effects
of UK firms to their international counterparts.
For this investigation, given the background discussion laid out,
the UK dataset is sufficiently comprehensive and policy-relevant.
\par
Over 100 Gb of raw data were accessed and downloaded from Fame in February 2025
by Professor Lars Nesheim for \cite{Irastorza-Fadrique2026} and reused for this project.
Raw data was ingested to \itx{DuckDB}, an open-source, MIT-licensed relational (SQL)
database architecture\footnote{\cite{Raasveldt2026}} optimised for high-compute variable manipulation.
This is crucial for the applications of $(n\times n)$ Matrix transformations
to a variable $n$-length column vector, as I detail heavily for calculating peer weights
and high-order transformations.
\par
Fame data has several limitations: impute errors means there are frequently
duplicate firm and financial observation entries, sometimes conflicting.
Fame has rich microdata observations with granular detail about each firm in the UK,
but it is not designed for spatial analysis. 
\par
Following data cleaning procedures discussed in \cite{Irastorza-Fadrique2026},
I removed duplicate entries to leave a single entry at random,
limited observations to those with more than 10 employees,
and observations with the required balance sheet properties, \itx{renumeration\_employees} and \itx{ebitda}
required to calculate firm GVA.
I detail the full ingestion and cleaning procedure in Appendix \ref{app:data-cleaning}.
The resulting sample of observations comprises exactly
152,379 firms and 1,128,490 panel observations.
Each of these has features of the relevant annual economic data of GVA, capital, and labour,
and 5 key location properties. The first set of these indicate membership of 3 spatial location groups:
travel-to-work area (\itx{ttwa})\footnote{Travel-to-work area definitions from 2011 are still considered
the mostly reliable for current analytical research,
due to the pollution in travel and working patterns resulting from the COVID-19 pandemic during the 2021 Census.
Travel-to-work-area area coverages are mostly time-invariant; changes between 2001 and 2011
were reasonably limited, \citep{OfficeforNationalStatistics2015}.},
the first-half of a UK postcode (\itx{pc4}), or a full UK postcode (\itx{pc8}).
The latter 2 are point distance decimals for longitude + latitude.

\subsection{Deriving panel aggregates}

All financial data are deflated to real terms, using 2024 as the base year.
I used macroeconomic data of the GDP deflator\footnote{\cite{ons2026:qna}} for all balance sheet variables,
except for \itx{renumeration\_employees} for which,
as income-related aggregates capturing household spending
pattern\footnote{\cite{ons2026:cpih-technical}},
I used CPIH\footnote{\cite{ons2026:cpih}}.
All financial data are in thousands of GBP (£'000s);
as I estimate log relationships exclusively, this has no effect on parameter interpretation.
\par
Labour input is taken from the number of employees reported on the FAME balance sheet per firm.
This dataset, like all others, does not include hours worked per employee on a firm-level that would offer a more
granular productivity (GVA per hour worked) measures. An estimation in this setting would likely have to impute the measure
from industry aggregates, which would likely confound the true firm-level signal.
\par
Capital is Fame's \itx{fixed\_total} variable, which represents the sum of tangibles, intangibles, and other investments
within the balance sheet, namely an extremely comprehensive measure of the firm's fixed capital.
The average capital-output ratio is 6x within FAME when filtering for positive values \tablerefp{tab:data-financial-ky},
which is moderately higher than macroeconomic aggregates typically suggest
likely due to accounting standard differences.

GVA is calculated as the sum of \itx{ebitda} and \itx{renumeration\_employees}.
\itx{renumeration\_employees} is the exact sum of \itx{wages}, \itx{social\_security\_costs},
and \itx{pensions\_costs} in the balance sheet.
\itx{ebitda} is in turn \itx{turnover} minus intermediate inputs and is
given in the Fame balance sheet for the vast majority of panel observations
in our dataset. It is also possible to reconstruct \itx{ebitda} via the sum of
\itx{profit\_loss\_pretax}, \itx{interest\_paid}, and \itx{depreciation},
providing an alternative measure of GVA.
We can expect the difference between these two to be accounting differences\footnote{
    An OLS regression between the two, discarding 5\% outliers, has a coefficient of 0.91 with a 0.000 p-value.
    This means \itx{gva2} is slightly lower than \itx{gva1}, with that impact mostly driven by top-end outliers.
}
and additionally note that the alternate GVA measure also reduces panel observations from 1,085,520 to 618,588.
For these reasons, I do not report TFP calculations with this second GVA measure
which are regardless near-identical, though I resultingly refer to GVA throughout this paper as \itx{gva\_1}.
\par
To help guide the choice of capital and output (GVA) measure, I conduct a quick benchmark
2-factor production estimation to help validate the choice of aggregates against known measurements.
\begin{align}
    \label{eqn:tfp2factor}
    y_{i,t} &= \alpha_i + \gamma_t + \beta_0
                + \beta_1 k_{i,t}
                + \beta_2 l_{i,t}
                + u_{i,t}
\end{align}
This specification does not yield consistent estimates when estimated with OLS,
according to the structural model I give in section \ref{ss:static-identification}.
$u_{i,t}$ is a non-structural error-residual correlated with $k_{i,t}, l_{i,t}$.
The estimation is a simple panel approach with time and fixed effects, and a constant.
Regressors are given in log terms to approximate a structural production function similar
to \eqref{eq:static-production-3-factor}.
Coefficients derived in \tablerefp{tab:tfp_fe} give a labour income share, $\hat\beta_2$,
exactly in line with ONS analysis of UK industry-level data
over the same time period, particularly \itx{corporate gross labour share} which is constructed
from a similar sample to the coverage in Fame\footnote{\cite{ons2024:labour-share-uk}}.

\par
As a demonstration, I include specifications which omit fixed effects.
The capital share of income jumps on a specification on the omission of firm fixed effects,
as all within-firm variation that we would model as the component $\alpha_i\in \varepsilon_{i,t}$,
the regression attributes to capital instead, which is reasonably consistent
in levels within a firm over time as it is a stock measure.
\par
When varying regressor measures, surprisingly,
running the regression with \itx{fixed\_total} as the capital measure instead of \itx{total\_assets}
almost entirely eliminates the capital share of income, or explanatory power of capital on variation in GVA.
Similar results are shown for a further decomposition of \itx{fixed\_total} to its asset components.
I leave it for future analysis why this dataset yields this phenomenon and
for the rest of the analysis, I use \itx{total\_assets} as the primary capital measure.
